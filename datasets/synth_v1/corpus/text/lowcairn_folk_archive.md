# Lowcairn Folk Archive

_A visitor's guide to the Lowcairn Folk Archive on Orrinhal, Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Anyone hoping to understand Orrinhal will sooner or later have to learn about the museum. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Travellers who arrive in Port Ambrel usually hear about the museum before they have even unpacked. The shop sells postcards, local honey and books by island authors. Opinions about the museum differ, but nobody on Orrinhal is indifferent to it. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Orrinhal as the museum.

It is fair to say that the museum has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Lowcairn. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Much of the material here was gathered during long autumn evenings in the reading room at Port Ambrel. Over the years the museum has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. The reading room of the Lowcairn Folk Archive is open to researchers by appointment. Some objects were donated by families who found them in attics and barns.

## Foundation

There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. It was in 1862 that the Lowcairn Folk Archive first opened to the public. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. The galleries of the Lowcairn Folk Archive are arranged around a small courtyard planted with herbs. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. School groups visit the Lowcairn Folk Archive regularly, and the education room is rarely empty. A small exhibition in Port Ambrel now displays photographs and tools from the period. A modest letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope.

Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. Several families from Port Ambrel claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. Historians on Orrinhal have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. The parish ledgers of Orrinhal record a long argument over who should pay for the work. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands.

## The collection

Labels are written in a friendly, unhurried style that suits the building. Visitors to the Lowcairn Folk Archive usually head straight for a painted wedding chest, its most famous object. Temporary exhibitions change each summer and often focus on a single craft. A volunteer team spends the winter cleaning, cataloguing and repairing objects. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Most houses in Lowcairn are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. The harbour at Lowcairn is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations.

From the high ground above Port Ambrel the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Orrinhal is a rugged island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. The Lowcairn Folk Archive displays its collection across 10 rooms. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop.

In summer the moorland above Port Ambrel turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each summer. The Lowcairn Folk Archive holds 3363 objects in its collection. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Low cloud can roll in from the west with very little warning. The light on Orrinhal changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time.

## Staff and volunteers

The curator of the Lowcairn Folk Archive is Una Mallowell. Wenna Quarhay, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first spring on the island. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Volunteers from Port Ambrel give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair.

There is a strong tradition of weaving, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. A noticeboard outside the shop in Port Ambrel lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. People in Lowcairn are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first.

## The building and its setting

In Port Ambrel it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. There is even a recipe for crab soup that is said to have been named after it. A popular local saying holds that Orrinhal has four seasons in a single afternoon. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every winter. Several folk songs from Port Ambrel mention it, usually in the last verse.

Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Lowcairn and the neighbouring parishes. Visiting writers have described it as patient, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name.

## Visiting

There is a small café nearby that serves oatcakes and strong tea. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Accommodation on Orrinhal is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards.

Entry to the Lowcairn Folk Archive costs 13.25 Saltmere crowns for adults. The walk from the harbour at Port Ambrel is pleasant but steep in places. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay.
