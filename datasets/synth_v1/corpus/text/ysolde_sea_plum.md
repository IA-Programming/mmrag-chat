# Ysolde sea plum

_Notes on Ysolde sea plum, a crop grown on Ysolde in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. This guide collects what is known about the Ysolde sea plum and its place in the life of Ysolde. Much of the material here was gathered during long spring evenings in the reading room at Merrow End. It is fair to say that the crop has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Ysolde Harbour. The harvest is still largely done by hand, with whole families joining in.

Agronomists from the mainland have studied the crop with growing interest. Some farmers swear by seaweed fertiliser, others by plain stubbornness. Islanders tend to describe the crop with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Market stalls in Ysolde Harbour are piled high with Ysolde sea plum when the season arrives. The story of the crop is tangled up with the story of Ysolde Harbour itself.

## History of cultivation

Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. Growers on Ysolde protect young Ysolde sea plum plants from the wind with low stone walls. The first grower of Ysolde sea plum was Quilla Thornmore, who farmed near Ysolde Harbour. Older residents of Merrow End still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. Historians on Ysolde have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. Old varieties of Ysolde sea plum are kept alive in a small seed bank run by volunteers. Several families from Merrow End claim an ancestor who played a part in those years.

## Growing

Surplus Ysolde sea plum is pressed, dried or turned into preserves for the winter. Growers bring in the Ysolde sea plum harvest in April. Cooks across the archipelago have their own favourite way of preparing Ysolde sea plum. The light on Ysolde changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. From the high ground above Merrow End the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Most houses in Ysolde Harbour are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate.

A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. When ripe, Ysolde sea plum takes on a smoky blue colour. Ysolde is a windswept island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan.

## Growers

The last harvest of Ysolde sea plum came to 7923 tonnes. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first spring on the island. Gideon Orraby, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. A noticeboard outside the shop in Ysolde Harbour lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. Many households combine several trades, such as weaving in winter and fishing in summer. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Ysolde Harbour by bus or, in bad weather, not at all.

Volunteers from Merrow End give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Darrow Talerby, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. People in Ysolde Harbour are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. There is a strong tradition of weaving, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table.

## Where it grows

Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each spring. The harbour at Merrow End is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. In autumn the moorland above Merrow End turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Gales can roll in from the west with very little warning.

A popular local saying holds that Ysolde has four seasons in a single afternoon. Most of the Ysolde sea plum crop comes from Ysolde. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. There is even a recipe for seed buns that is said to have been named after it. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop.

## Buying and eating

At market Ysolde sea plum sells for about 4.25 Saltmere crowns per kilogram. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Ysolde Harbour on most mornings in spring. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. There is a small café nearby that serves crab soup and strong tea.

The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Accommodation on Ysolde is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill.

## Notes

Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. In Ysolde Harbour it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Visiting writers have described it as sheltered, a word the islanders have happily adopted. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Merrow End and the neighbouring parishes. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining.

Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every winter. Several folk songs from Ysolde Harbour mention it, usually in the last verse. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Ysolde Harbour. Local guidebooks tend to give the crop only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice.
