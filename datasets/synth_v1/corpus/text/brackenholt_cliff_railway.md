# Brackenholt Cliff Railway

_A guide to the Brackenholt Cliff Railway, a heritage railway on Brackenholt in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Steam excursions on the Brackenholt Cliff Railway run on selected weekends and are booked out quickly. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the railway still stands out. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Volunteers maintain the track, the stations and the small engine shed. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date.

Islanders tend to describe the railway with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Opinions about the railway differ, but nobody on Brackenholt is indifferent to it. The story of the railway is tangled up with the story of Wenmouth itself. It is fair to say that the railway has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Crowle Heath.

## Building the line

A sheltered letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. Passengers often lean out of the windows to enjoy the views over the bay. The Brackenholt Cliff Railway was designed by the engineer Darrow Marrquist. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. A small museum at the terminus displays signals, lamps and timetables.

The parish ledgers of Brackenholt record a long argument over who should pay for the work. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Passenger services on the Brackenholt Cliff Railway began in 1950. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. Several families from Wenmouth claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. Historians on Brackenholt have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night.

## Engines and carriages

Sheep on the line are a regular cause of short delays. The ticket office still issues old-fashioned card tickets. The oldest engine on the Brackenholt Cliff Railway is called Captain Hollis. The line was built to carry stone and fish, and passengers were an afterthought. The carriages of the Brackenholt Cliff Railway are painted in a livery that has barely changed in living memory. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each winter. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms.

Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. The top speed on the Brackenholt Cliff Railway is 16 km/h. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Sea fog can roll in from the west with very little warning. The light on Brackenholt changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. In autumn the moorland above Crowle Heath turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops.

## Staff and volunteers

Rafe Fennington, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. A noticeboard outside the shop in Wenmouth lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Wenmouth by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. There is a strong tradition of dry-stone walling, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Many households combine several trades, such as rope making in winter and fishing in summer. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return.

## The route

The harbour at Wenmouth is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. End to end, the Brackenholt Cliff Railway measures 9.8 km. From the high ground above Crowle Heath the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Brackenholt is a remote island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Most houses in Wenmouth are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining.

Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. The Brackenholt Cliff Railway serves 22 stations along its route. Several folk songs from Crowle Heath mention it, usually in the last verse. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Crowle Heath. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Wenmouth and the neighbouring parishes.

## Riding the line

Accommodation on Brackenholt is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. There is a small café nearby that serves barley bread and strong tea. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth.

Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Crowle Heath on most mornings in winter. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites.

## Notes

Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. There is even a recipe for oatcakes that is said to have been named after it. In Crowle Heath it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Visiting writers have described it as proud, a word the islanders have happily adopted.

The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. A popular local saying holds that Brackenholt has four seasons in a single afternoon. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every spring. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Brackenholt as the railway. Travellers who arrive in Wenmouth usually hear about the railway before they have even unpacked.
