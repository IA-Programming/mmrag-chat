# Orrinhal Point Lighthouse (archived 2019 edition)

_A guide to the Orrinhal Point Lighthouse, the lighthouse of Orrinhal in the Saltmere Archipelago._

_Archived 2019 edition, kept for reference only. Superseded by the revised 2024 edition._

## Overview

Islanders tend to describe the lighthouse with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Fishing crews heading home use the Orrinhal Point Lighthouse as a reassurance as much as a guide. Local guidebooks tend to give the lighthouse only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Much of the material here was gathered during long spring evenings in the reading room at Port Ambrel. Opinions about the lighthouse differ, but nobody on Orrinhal is indifferent to it.

The lamp room of the Orrinhal Point Lighthouse is reached through a narrow trapdoor that tests the knees of every visitor. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. The story of the lighthouse is tangled up with the story of Port Ambrel itself. This guide collects what is known about the Orrinhal Point Lighthouse and its place in the life of Orrinhal. It is fair to say that the lighthouse has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Lowcairn. Travellers who arrive in Port Ambrel usually hear about the lighthouse before they have even unpacked. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date.

## History of the light

In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Several families from Lowcairn claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. A stubborn letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope.

Older residents of Port Ambrel still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. Inside, the walls are lined with painted boards recording past keepers. The fog signal is sounded only rarely these days, but the islanders still remember its voice. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. A small exhibition in Port Ambrel now displays photographs and tools from the period.

## The tower

The keepers' cottages at the foot of the tower now house a small exhibition. Salt spray has worn the lower courses of stone into soft, rounded shapes. Climbing to the lamp room of the Orrinhal Point Lighthouse means tackling 186 steps on a tight spiral staircase. (Figure as printed in the archived 2019 edition.) A brass logbook in the watch room of the Orrinhal Point Lighthouse records every ship that has passed within sight. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. On stormy nights the beam sweeps across the water in a slow, steady rhythm. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm.

Most houses in Lowcairn are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. The harbour at Port Ambrel is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. From the high ground above Lowcairn the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms.

## Keepers and neighbours

Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. There is a strong tradition of basketry, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Volunteers from Port Ambrel give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair. Many households combine several trades, such as net mending in winter and fishing in summer. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first autumn on the island.

## The coast around Orrinhal

Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Orrinhal is a proud island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Low cloud can roll in from the west with very little warning. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn.

It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Port Ambrel and the neighbouring parishes. The light on Orrinhal changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each winter. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. In spring the moorland above Lowcairn turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops.

## Visiting

Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. There is a small café nearby that serves crab soup and strong tea. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain.

Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. Accommodation on Orrinhal is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. The walk from the harbour at Lowcairn is pleasant but steep in places.

## Stories and notes

Visiting writers have described it as patient, a word the islanders have happily adopted. In Port Ambrel it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Several folk songs from Port Ambrel mention it, usually in the last verse.

Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every winter. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Port Ambrel. A popular local saying holds that Orrinhal has four seasons in a single afternoon. There is even a recipe for plum cake that is said to have been named after it.
