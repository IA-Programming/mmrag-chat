# Tamsin Shoal Shuttle

_A traveller's guide to the Tamsin Shoal Shuttle, the ferry route serving Tamsin in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Much of the material here was gathered during long summer evenings in the reading room at Brightwick. Local guidebooks tend to give the ferry route only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Passengers on the Tamsin Shoal Shuttle share the deck with crates of post, groceries and the occasional sheep. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Sorrel Bay. Opinions about the ferry route differ, but nobody on Tamsin is indifferent to it.

The crew know many of the regular passengers by name. It is fair to say that the ferry route has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Sorrel Bay. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the ferry route still stands out. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. In rough weather the crossing is cancelled, and the harbour café fills with stranded travellers. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Travellers who arrive in Sorrel Bay usually hear about the ferry route before they have even unpacked.

## History of the route

During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. Several families from Sorrel Bay claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. Cars must be booked in advance, while foot passengers can usually just turn up. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. The parish ledgers of Tamsin record a long argument over who should pay for the work.

## On board

Timetables for the Tamsin Shoal Shuttle are posted on the harbour office door and change with the seasons. A crossing on the Tamsin Shoal Shuttle takes 126 minutes. A small saloon below deck serves tea, soup and sandwiches. Dolphins sometimes follow the boat for part of the way. Islanders depend on the Tamsin Shoal Shuttle for everything from medicine to newspapers. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. In spring the moorland above Sorrel Bay turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops.

Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. The vessel MV Saltheart has worked the Tamsin Shoal Shuttle for many seasons. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Sea fog can roll in from the west with very little warning. Tamsin is a salt-stained island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

## Crew and passengers

Elspeth Mallowhay is the senior captain on the Tamsin Shoal Shuttle. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Brightwick by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Kit Zellowby, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Vesper Kettmarrow, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations.

Many households combine several trades, such as stone carving in winter and fishing in summer. Volunteers from Brightwick give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. A noticeboard outside the shop in Brightwick lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. Conversations in Sorrel Bay often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of kelp broth. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate.

## The waters

The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Brightwick is the departure port for the Tamsin Shoal Shuttle. The harbour at Sorrel Bay is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Most houses in Sorrel Bay are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate.

The route of the Tamsin Shoal Shuttle measures 91 km from quay to quay. From the high ground above Sorrel Bay the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. The light on Tamsin changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn. In Sorrel Bay it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea.

## Tickets and travel

Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. The walk from the harbour at Sorrel Bay is pleasant but steep in places. A single adult ticket on the Tamsin Shoal Shuttle costs 4.25 Saltmere crowns. Accommodation on Tamsin is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office.

Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay.
