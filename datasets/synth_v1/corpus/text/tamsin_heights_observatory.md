# Tamsin Heights Observatory

_A guide to the Tamsin Heights Observatory, the observatory on Tamsin in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Islanders tend to describe the observatory with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Anyone hoping to understand Tamsin will sooner or later have to learn about the observatory. This guide collects what is known about the Tamsin Heights Observatory and its place in the life of Tamsin. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Sorrel Bay.

Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Travellers who arrive in Sorrel Bay usually hear about the observatory before they have even unpacked. A small library at the Tamsin Heights Observatory holds star atlases, logbooks and hand-drawn charts of the night sky. Students from across the archipelago compete for a few nights of observing time each year. The Tamsin Heights Observatory keeps a strict rule against bright lights on the access road after dusk. Clear, dark skies are the main reason the site was chosen.

## Origins

The early accounts mention a modest wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. Astronomers at the Tamsin Heights Observatory share their data with partner institutes on the mainland. It was in 2006 that the main mirror of the Tamsin Heights Observatory was finally installed. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. Historians on Tamsin have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs.

## Instruments

The dome opens with a long metallic groan that can be heard far down the valley. A 3.1 m mirror, ground from one blank of glass, sits at the heart of the main telescope of the Tamsin Heights Observatory. The control room smells faintly of coffee, machine oil and old paper. Public open nights are popular, especially when a bright planet is well placed. Most houses in Brightwick are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm.

The light on Tamsin changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. The largest instrument at the Tamsin Heights Observatory is affectionately nicknamed the Long Tom. The harbour at Sorrel Bay is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each summer. From the high ground above Sorrel Bay the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away.

A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. The dome of the Tamsin Heights Observatory measures 9.1 m across. In autumn the moorland above Sorrel Bay turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Sudden hail can roll in from the west with very little warning.

## The site

Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Records kept at the Tamsin Heights Observatory show an average of 188 clear nights each year. Tamsin is a rugged island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every summer. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Brightwick.

Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. There is even a recipe for oatcakes that is said to have been named after it. Several folk songs from Sorrel Bay mention it, usually in the last verse. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. In Sorrel Bay it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects.

## Staff and students

People in Sorrel Bay are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. Many households combine several trades, such as weaving in winter and fishing in summer. The Tamsin Heights Observatory is led by director Teodor Branisk, who also teaches at the island college. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. There is a strong tradition of weaving, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends.

## Open nights

Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening.

## Miscellany

Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Visiting writers have described it as proud, a word the islanders have happily adopted. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Brightwick and the neighbouring parishes. A popular local saying holds that Tamsin has four seasons in a single afternoon.

Over the years the observatory has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. The story of the observatory is tangled up with the story of Sorrel Bay itself. Much of the material here was gathered during long spring evenings in the reading room at Brightwick. It is fair to say that the observatory has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Brightwick. Opinions about the observatory differ, but nobody on Tamsin is indifferent to it. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the observatory still stands out.
