# Corvane Crossing (revised 2024 edition)

_A traveller's guide to the Corvane Crossing, the ferry route serving Corvane in the Saltmere Archipelago._

_Revised 2024 edition. This edition supersedes the archived 2019 edition._

## Overview

Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Passengers on the Corvane Crossing share the deck with crates of post, groceries and the occasional sheep. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Dunlin. Over the years the ferry route has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the ferry route still stands out. Cars must be booked in advance, while foot passengers can usually just turn up.

Much of the material here was gathered during long autumn evenings in the reading room at Corvane Quay. Opinions about the ferry route differ, but nobody on Corvane is indifferent to it. It is fair to say that the ferry route has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Dunlin. Anyone hoping to understand Corvane will sooner or later have to learn about the ferry route. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Corvane as the ferry route. The story of the ferry route is tangled up with the story of Corvane Quay itself.

## History of the route

The crew know many of the regular passengers by name. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. Dolphins sometimes follow the boat for part of the way. Older residents of Dunlin still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night.

Islanders depend on the Corvane Crossing for everything from medicine to newspapers. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. The parish ledgers of Corvane record a long argument over who should pay for the work. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. Historians on Corvane have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs.

## On board

A small saloon below deck serves tea, soup and sandwiches. Timetables for the Corvane Crossing are posted on the harbour office door and change with the seasons. The vessel MV Lantern Bay has worked the Corvane Crossing for many seasons. In rough weather the crossing is cancelled, and the harbour café fills with stranded travellers. From the high ground above Dunlin the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Sea fog can roll in from the west with very little warning.

A crossing on the Corvane Crossing takes 57 minutes. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. The harbour at Corvane Quay is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Corvane is a unhurried island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Most houses in Corvane Quay are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate.

## Crew and passengers

Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Corvane Quay by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. There is a strong tradition of dry-stone walling, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Many households combine several trades, such as knitting in winter and fishing in summer. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Conversations in Dunlin often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of seed buns.

## The waters

Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each winter. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Boats on the Corvane Crossing leave from Corvane Quay. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather.

The Corvane Crossing covers 107 km of open water. The light on Corvane changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. In autumn the moorland above Dunlin turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Dunlin. Visiting writers have described it as rugged, a word the islanders have happily adopted. There is even a recipe for crab soup that is said to have been named after it.

## Tickets and travel

Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Dunlin on most mornings in winter. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. The Corvane Crossing charges 9.50 Saltmere crowns for a single adult ticket. (Figure checked for the revised 2024 edition.) The walk from the harbour at Corvane Quay is pleasant but steep in places. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Accommodation on Corvane is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay.

The Corvane Crossing runs 3 departures a day during the summer timetable. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. There is a small café nearby that serves barley bread and strong tea.

## Notes

Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Several folk songs from Corvane Quay mention it, usually in the last verse. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects.
