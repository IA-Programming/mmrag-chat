# Halvric Night Ferry

_A traveller's guide to the Halvric Night Ferry, the ferry route serving Halvric in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Over the years the ferry route has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Halvric as the ferry route. Anyone hoping to understand Halvric will sooner or later have to learn about the ferry route. The story of the ferry route is tangled up with the story of Stoneacre itself. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the ferry route still stands out. Islanders tend to describe the ferry route with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation.

Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Opinions about the ferry route differ, but nobody on Halvric is indifferent to it. A small saloon below deck serves tea, soup and sandwiches. Much of the material here was gathered during long winter evenings in the reading room at Stoneacre. Local guidebooks tend to give the ferry route only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Stoneacre. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling.

## History of the route

The crew know many of the regular passengers by name. Historians on Halvric have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. A small exhibition in Halvric Bay now displays photographs and tools from the period. Older residents of Halvric Bay still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days.

Passengers on the Halvric Night Ferry share the deck with crates of post, groceries and the occasional sheep. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. A quiet letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough.

## On board

Cars must be booked in advance, while foot passengers can usually just turn up. Dolphins sometimes follow the boat for part of the way. In rough weather the crossing is cancelled, and the harbour café fills with stranded travellers. Islanders depend on the Halvric Night Ferry for everything from medicine to newspapers. Timetables for the Halvric Night Ferry are posted on the harbour office door and change with the seasons. From the high ground above Stoneacre the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. The harbour at Halvric Bay is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations.

Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each spring. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. A crossing on the Halvric Night Ferry takes 178 minutes. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Most houses in Halvric Bay are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. In autumn the moorland above Stoneacre turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather.

## Crew and passengers

Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Stoneacre by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. There is a strong tradition of weaving, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Una Nethwake, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends.

Una Branmere is the senior captain on the Halvric Night Ferry. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Conversations in Stoneacre often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of kelp broth. People in Stoneacre are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first spring on the island. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate.

## The waters

Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. The Halvric Night Ferry covers 44 km of open water. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop.

Halvric is a modest island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Stoneacre is the departure port for the Halvric Night Ferry. Squalls can roll in from the west with very little warning. The light on Halvric changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. In Stoneacre it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day.

## Tickets and travel

Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Stoneacre on most mornings in summer. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. A single adult ticket on the Halvric Night Ferry costs 21.25 Saltmere crowns. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. The walk from the harbour at Stoneacre is pleasant but steep in places.

Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. In summer the Halvric Night Ferry offers 6 departures each day. There is a small café nearby that serves oatcakes and strong tea. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Accommodation on Halvric is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill.

## Notes

Several folk songs from Stoneacre mention it, usually in the last verse. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Halvric Bay and the neighbouring parishes. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves.

A popular local saying holds that Halvric has four seasons in a single afternoon. Visiting writers have described it as quiet, a word the islanders have happily adopted. There is even a recipe for mutton stew that is said to have been named after it. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Stoneacre.
