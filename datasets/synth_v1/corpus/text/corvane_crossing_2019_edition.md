# Corvane Crossing (archived 2019 edition)

_A traveller's guide to the Corvane Crossing, the ferry route serving Corvane in the Saltmere Archipelago._

_Archived 2019 edition, kept for reference only. Superseded by the revised 2024 edition._

## Overview

Passengers on the Corvane Crossing share the deck with crates of post, groceries and the occasional sheep. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the ferry route still stands out. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Cars must be booked in advance, while foot passengers can usually just turn up. Over the years the ferry route has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Dunlin.

Much of the material here was gathered during long autumn evenings in the reading room at Corvane Quay. Opinions about the ferry route differ, but nobody on Corvane is indifferent to it. The story of the ferry route is tangled up with the story of Corvane Quay itself. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Corvane as the ferry route. Anyone hoping to understand Corvane will sooner or later have to learn about the ferry route. It is fair to say that the ferry route has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Dunlin.

## History of the route

Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. Older residents of Dunlin still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. The crew know many of the regular passengers by name. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Dolphins sometimes follow the boat for part of the way.

Historians on Corvane have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. The parish ledgers of Corvane record a long argument over who should pay for the work. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. Islanders depend on the Corvane Crossing for everything from medicine to newspapers.

## On board

From the high ground above Dunlin the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. A small saloon below deck serves tea, soup and sandwiches. Timetables for the Corvane Crossing are posted on the harbour office door and change with the seasons. Sea fog can roll in from the west with very little warning. In rough weather the crossing is cancelled, and the harbour café fills with stranded travellers.

The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. The harbour at Corvane Quay is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Corvane is a unhurried island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Most houses in Corvane Quay are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate.

## Crew and passengers

Many households combine several trades, such as knitting in winter and fishing in summer. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Corvane Quay by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. There is a strong tradition of dry-stone walling, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Conversations in Dunlin often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of seed buns.

## The waters

Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each winter. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm.

A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Dunlin. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. There is even a recipe for crab soup that is said to have been named after it. In autumn the moorland above Dunlin turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Visiting writers have described it as rugged, a word the islanders have happily adopted. The light on Corvane changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time.

## Tickets and travel

Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Dunlin on most mornings in winter. A single adult ticket on the Corvane Crossing costs 6.75 Saltmere crowns. (Figure as printed in the archived 2019 edition.) Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. The walk from the harbour at Corvane Quay is pleasant but steep in places. Accommodation on Corvane is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards.

Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. There is a small café nearby that serves barley bread and strong tea. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office.

## Notes

Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Several folk songs from Corvane Quay mention it, usually in the last verse. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves.
