# Saltwing Ferry Line

_A traveller's guide to the Saltwing Ferry Line, the ferry route serving Kestrelmoor in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Kestrelmoor as the ferry route. Much of the material here was gathered during long spring evenings in the reading room at Gullstead. Islanders tend to describe the ferry route with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Local guidebooks tend to give the ferry route only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Anyone hoping to understand Kestrelmoor will sooner or later have to learn about the ferry route. The story of the ferry route is tangled up with the story of Gullstead itself. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the ferry route still stands out.

The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Hollinmere. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. This guide collects what is known about the Saltwing Ferry Line and its place in the life of Kestrelmoor. It is fair to say that the ferry route has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Gullstead. Travellers who arrive in Hollinmere usually hear about the ferry route before they have even unpacked.

## History of the route

The parish ledgers of Kestrelmoor record a long argument over who should pay for the work. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Several families from Gullstead claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good.

## On board

Timetables for the Saltwing Ferry Line are posted on the harbour office door and change with the seasons. In rough weather the crossing is cancelled, and the harbour café fills with stranded travellers. Passengers on the Saltwing Ferry Line spend 149 minutes at sea on a typical crossing. A small saloon below deck serves tea, soup and sandwiches. The crew know many of the regular passengers by name. Islanders depend on the Saltwing Ferry Line for everything from medicine to newspapers. Dolphins sometimes follow the boat for part of the way.

Cars must be booked in advance, while foot passengers can usually just turn up. Passengers on the Saltwing Ferry Line share the deck with crates of post, groceries and the occasional sheep. The vessel MV Morrow Star has worked the Saltwing Ferry Line for many seasons. In summer the moorland above Hollinmere turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Low cloud can roll in from the west with very little warning. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm.

## Crew and passengers

Many households combine several trades, such as knitting in winter and fishing in summer. People in Hollinmere are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Petra Talworth, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends.

Conversations in Hollinmere often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of smoked fish. Brisa Zellmere is the senior captain on the Saltwing Ferry Line. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Anouk Dravaby, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. There is a strong tradition of knitting, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first summer on the island. Volunteers from Hollinmere give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair.

## The waters

Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. The harbour at Hollinmere is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each summer. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop.

From the high ground above Gullstead the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. The light on Kestrelmoor changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Most houses in Gullstead are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Kestrelmoor is a rugged island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

## Tickets and travel

Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Accommodation on Kestrelmoor is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Hollinmere on most mornings in winter. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites.

The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. A single adult ticket on the Saltwing Ferry Line costs 10.75 Saltmere crowns. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. The walk from the harbour at Gullstead is pleasant but steep in places.

## Notes

In Gullstead it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Gullstead and the neighbouring parishes. Several folk songs from Hollinmere mention it, usually in the last verse. There is even a recipe for oatcakes that is said to have been named after it. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop.
