# Saffren Bloom Fair

_A guide to the Saffren Bloom Fair, held on Saffren in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Travellers who arrive in Saffren Cross usually hear about the festival before they have even unpacked. Islanders tend to describe the festival with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Opinions about the festival differ, but nobody on Saffren is indifferent to it. Over the years the festival has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the festival still stands out. Preparations for the Saffren Bloom Fair begin months in advance in sheds and kitchens across the island.

Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Saffren as the festival. It is fair to say that the festival has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Saffren Cross. Anyone hoping to understand Saffren will sooner or later have to learn about the festival. Local guidebooks tend to give the festival only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Much of the material here was gathered during long autumn evenings in the reading room at Saffren Cross. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Pellby.

## Origins

The first Saffren Bloom Fair took place in 1817. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. Older residents of Saffren Cross still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. A stubborn letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. On the final evening a procession winds its way from the chapel down to the quay. Several families from Pellby claim an ancestor who played a part in those years.

## The programme

Families who have moved away often return specially for the Saffren Bloom Fair. Celebrations at the Saffren Bloom Fair last 5 days. Brass bands, fiddlers and choirs take turns on the small stage by the harbour wall. Visitors are encouraged to join in the dancing, however clumsy their steps. Stalls sell hot food, hand-made toys and souvenirs of every description. Rain rarely stops the celebrations, although it sometimes shortens the speeches. The committee behind the Saffren Bloom Fair meets weekly through the winter to plan the programme.

Every October, the Saffren Bloom Fair takes over the streets of Saffren Cross. The harbour at Pellby is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. From the high ground above Saffren Cross the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Saffren is a unhurried island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn.

## Who takes part

Isidore Sallrey, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first autumn on the island. The Saffren Bloom Fair is organised by Darrow Zellraith and a committee of volunteers. A noticeboard outside the shop in Pellby lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. Many households combine several trades, such as basketry in winter and fishing in summer. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Conversations in Saffren Cross often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of crab soup. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable.

## Setting

The light on Saffren changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. In winter the moorland above Saffren Cross turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish.

Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Most houses in Pellby are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each spring. Squalls can roll in from the west with very little warning. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Pellby. There is even a recipe for oatcakes that is said to have been named after it.

## Practical information

Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. The traditional dish of the Saffren Bloom Fair is gorse-flower fritters. There is a small café nearby that serves smoked fish and strong tea. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Pellby on most mornings in winter. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths.

## Traditions

It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Pellby and the neighbouring parishes. Several folk songs from Pellby mention it, usually in the last verse. The emblem of the Saffren Bloom Fair is a sleeping fox. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every spring. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Visiting writers have described it as windswept, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name.

In Saffren Cross it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. A popular local saying holds that Saffren has four seasons in a single afternoon. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining.
