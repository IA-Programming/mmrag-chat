# Brindle Robotics

_A profile of Brindle Robotics, a manufacturer based on Orrinhal in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Opinions about the company differ, but nobody on Orrinhal is indifferent to it. Over the years the company has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. The company sponsors the local football team and the annual regatta. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Lowcairn.

Anyone hoping to understand Orrinhal will sooner or later have to learn about the company. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Orrinhal as the company. Much of the material here was gathered during long spring evenings in the reading room at Port Ambrel. Local guidebooks tend to give the company only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Travellers who arrive in Lowcairn usually hear about the company before they have even unpacked. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. A small showroom near the harbour displays early prototypes alongside the current range.

## Beginnings

A small exhibition in Lowcairn now displays photographs and tools from the period. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. Most of the components are still assembled by hand in the main hall. The parish ledgers of Orrinhal record a long argument over who should pay for the work. Customers on the mainland have only recently begun to notice its products.

Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. The founder of Brindle Robotics, Nell Caelington, started the business in a converted boathouse. Several families from Port Ambrel claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. A modest letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night.

## Products

Managers at Brindle Robotics insist that quality matters more than speed. Brindle Robotics began as a small workshop and still keeps a workbench from those early days. Staff often describe the company culture as practical, frugal and a little stubborn. Apprentices from the island school regularly join Brindle Robotics after finishing their training. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop.

Brindle Robotics is best known for the Corvid-9 survey drone. Sea fog can roll in from the west with very little warning. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. From the high ground above Lowcairn the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Most houses in Lowcairn are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. The harbour at Port Ambrel is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather.

## People

Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Vesper Sallington, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Some 1434 people work for Brindle Robotics, most of them in the main workshop. Sabine Dunworth, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. People in Port Ambrel are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable.

Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first summer on the island. Conversations in Lowcairn often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of smoked fish. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Lowcairn by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. There is a strong tradition of boatbuilding, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Many households combine several trades, such as rope making in winter and fishing in summer.

## Home town

Brindle Robotics has its headquarters in Lowcairn, a short walk from the harbour. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn. The light on Orrinhal changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. In spring the moorland above Lowcairn turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Orrinhal is a unhurried island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. In Lowcairn it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every winter. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining.

## Visiting the works

Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. The walk from the harbour at Port Ambrel is pleasant but steep in places. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain.

## Notes

Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Above the door of Brindle Robotics hangs its painted motto: "Steady hands, salt air". Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Lowcairn and the neighbouring parishes. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves.

Several folk songs from Port Ambrel mention it, usually in the last verse. There is even a recipe for barley bread that is said to have been named after it. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Lowcairn. A popular local saying holds that Orrinhal has four seasons in a single afternoon. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Visiting writers have described it as modest, a word the islanders have happily adopted.
