# Saltmere ibis

_A field guide entry for the Saltmere ibis, a bird found on Thistlewane in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Over the years the bird has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Thistle Hythe. It is fair to say that the bird has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Thistle Hythe. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. This guide collects what is known about the Saltmere ibis and its place in the life of Thistlewane. The story of the bird is tangled up with the story of Thistle Hythe itself.

Anyone hoping to understand Thistlewane will sooner or later have to learn about the bird. Travellers who arrive in Larkspur usually hear about the bird before they have even unpacked. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the bird still stands out. The Saltmere ibis is shy, and patient observers are rewarded more often than noisy ones. Local guidebooks tend to give the bird only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Islanders tend to describe the bird with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred.

## Discovery

The Saltmere ibis was first described by the naturalist Rafe Thornhay. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Several families from Larkspur claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands.

In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. Gulls and stoats are a constant threat to its young. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. Older residents of Thistle Hythe still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. Historians on Thistlewane have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs.

## Appearance and habits

A wild Saltmere ibis can live for up to 25 years. Photographers often wait for hours in hides to capture a good picture of it. Conservation volunteers count the Saltmere ibis each summer along standard survey routes. Naturalists on Thistlewane have kept careful notes on its behaviour for generations. The bird is protected, and disturbing its nesting sites is an offence. Children on the island learn to recognise the Saltmere ibis on school nature walks. Its call is often heard at dusk, carrying far across the water.

The Saltmere ibis has a wingspan of about 119 cm. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Thistlewane is a remote island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish.

Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Clutches of the Saltmere ibis usually hold 3 eggs. In summer the moorland above Thistle Hythe turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. The harbour at Larkspur is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms.

## Watchers and researchers

Zeno Zelligane, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first autumn on the island. People in Thistle Hythe are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. There is a strong tradition of dry-stone walling, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table.

## Habitat

The Saltmere ibis nests mainly in sea caves on the northern cliffs. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. From the high ground above Larkspur the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Gales can roll in from the west with very little warning. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each winter. Most houses in Thistle Hythe are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. The light on Thistlewane changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Several folk songs from Larkspur mention it, usually in the last verse.

The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. A popular local saying holds that Thistlewane has four seasons in a single afternoon. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day.

## Watching it

Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Thistle Hythe on most mornings in winter. There is a small café nearby that serves barley bread and strong tea. Accommodation on Thistlewane is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths.

Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office.

## Folklore

Visiting writers have described it as salt-stained, a word the islanders have happily adopted. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Larkspur. In Thistle Hythe it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. There is even a recipe for smoked fish that is said to have been named after it. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it.

The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every summer. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Larkspur and the neighbouring parishes. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Thistlewane as the bird. Opinions about the bird differ, but nobody on Thistlewane is indifferent to it. Much of the material here was gathered during long spring evenings in the reading room at Thistle Hythe.
