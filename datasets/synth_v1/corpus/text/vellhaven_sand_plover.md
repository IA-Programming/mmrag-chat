# Vellhaven sand plover

_A field guide entry for the Vellhaven sand plover, a bird found on Vellhaven in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Conservation volunteers count the Vellhaven sand plover each winter along standard survey routes. Photographers often wait for hours in hides to capture a good picture of it. This guide collects what is known about the Vellhaven sand plover and its place in the life of Vellhaven. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. It is fair to say that the bird has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Marrowgate.

Anyone hoping to understand Vellhaven will sooner or later have to learn about the bird. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Vellhaven as the bird. The story of the bird is tangled up with the story of Marrowgate itself. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the bird still stands out. Opinions about the bird differ, but nobody on Vellhaven is indifferent to it. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Marrowgate. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred.

## Discovery

During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. A patient letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Naturalists on Vellhaven have kept careful notes on its behaviour for generations. Children on the island learn to recognise the Vellhaven sand plover on school nature walks. Older residents of Calloway still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough.

## Appearance and habits

The Vellhaven sand plover is shy, and patient observers are rewarded more often than noisy ones. In the wild, the Vellhaven sand plover lives as long as 27 years. The bird is protected, and disturbing its nesting sites is an offence. Gulls and stoats are a constant threat to its young. Its call is often heard at dusk, carrying far across the water. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish.

From beak to tail, an adult Vellhaven sand plover reaches about 38 cm. The harbour at Calloway is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. In winter the moorland above Marrowgate turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each spring. From the high ground above Calloway the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day.

Vellhaven is a windswept island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Squalls can roll in from the west with very little warning. Clutches of the Vellhaven sand plover usually hold 4 eggs. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. The light on Vellhaven changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time.

## Watchers and researchers

Conversations in Marrowgate often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of crab soup. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. A noticeboard outside the shop in Marrowgate lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. Volunteers from Marrowgate give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair.

Surveyors recorded 2893 breeding pairs of the Vellhaven sand plover at the last count. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. There is a strong tradition of stone carving, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Calloway by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. Veda Marroch, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first summer on the island.

## Habitat

Most houses in Calloway are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. A popular local saying holds that Vellhaven has four seasons in a single afternoon. In Calloway it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day.

Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Calloway and the neighbouring parishes. Visiting writers have described it as salt-stained, a word the islanders have happily adopted. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Marrowgate. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every spring. Several folk songs from Calloway mention it, usually in the last verse.

## Watching it

Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth.

The walk from the harbour at Calloway is pleasant but steep in places. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. There is a small café nearby that serves crab soup and strong tea. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Calloway on most mornings in winter. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Accommodation on Vellhaven is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season.

## Folklore

Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. There is even a recipe for oatcakes that is said to have been named after it. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop.
