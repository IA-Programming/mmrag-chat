# Quillmere Clock Museum (revised 2024 edition)

_A visitor's guide to the Quillmere Clock Museum on Quillmere, Saltmere Archipelago._

_Revised 2024 edition. This edition supersedes the archived 2019 edition._

## Overview

Islanders tend to describe the museum with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Temporary exhibitions change each summer and often focus on a single craft. The story of the museum is tangled up with the story of Tollhaven itself. It is fair to say that the museum has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Tollhaven. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Over the years the museum has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago.

Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the museum still stands out. Travellers who arrive in Tollhaven usually hear about the museum before they have even unpacked. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Opinions about the museum differ, but nobody on Quillmere is indifferent to it. Local guidebooks tend to give the museum only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice.

## Foundation

Several families from Ashcombe claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. The Quillmere Clock Museum opened its doors in 1908. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. A sheltered letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. The parish ledgers of Quillmere record a long argument over who should pay for the work. The early accounts mention a weathered wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. A small exhibition in Ashcombe now displays photographs and tools from the period.

## The collection

A volunteer team spends the winter cleaning, cataloguing and repairing objects. The reading room of the Quillmere Clock Museum is open to researchers by appointment. The galleries of the Quillmere Clock Museum are arranged around a small courtyard planted with herbs. Labels are written in a friendly, unhurried style that suits the building. Some objects were donated by families who found them in attics and barns. School groups visit the Quillmere Clock Museum regularly, and the education room is rarely empty. The shop sells postcards, local honey and books by island authors.

Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. From the high ground above Ashcombe the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. In winter the moorland above Ashcombe turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather.

## Staff and volunteers

There is a strong tradition of weaving, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Elspeth Luserby has been curator of the Quillmere Clock Museum for over a decade. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Ashcombe by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. A noticeboard outside the shop in Ashcombe lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. Conversations in Tollhaven often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of kelp broth. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first spring on the island.

About 47,565 visitors pass through the Quillmere Clock Museum every year. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. People in Ashcombe are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Volunteers from Ashcombe give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair.

## The building and its setting

Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. The harbour at Tollhaven is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Quillmere is a salt-stained island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

The light on Quillmere changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn. Squalls can roll in from the west with very little warning. Most houses in Ashcombe are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop.

## Visiting

Entry to the Quillmere Clock Museum costs 8.25 Saltmere crowns for adults. (Figure checked for the revised 2024 edition.) The walk from the harbour at Tollhaven is pleasant but steep in places. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Accommodation on Quillmere is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites.

The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Tollhaven on most mornings in spring. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out.

## Notes

Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Ashcombe. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves.

In Tollhaven it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Several folk songs from Tollhaven mention it, usually in the last verse. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Ashcombe and the neighbouring parishes. A popular local saying holds that Quillmere has four seasons in a single afternoon.
