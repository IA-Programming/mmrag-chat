# Lantern Tide Festival

_A guide to the Lantern Tide Festival, held on Orrinhal in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Local guidebooks tend to give the festival only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the festival still stands out. Opinions about the festival differ, but nobody on Orrinhal is indifferent to it. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Port Ambrel. The story of the festival is tangled up with the story of Lowcairn itself. Over the years the festival has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago.

Islanders tend to describe the festival with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Rain rarely stops the celebrations, although it sometimes shortens the speeches. It is fair to say that the festival has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Port Ambrel. Preparations for the Lantern Tide Festival begin months in advance in sheds and kitchens across the island. This guide collects what is known about the Lantern Tide Festival and its place in the life of Orrinhal.

## Origins

The first Lantern Tide Festival took place in 1829. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. Stalls sell hot food, hand-made toys and souvenirs of every description. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. The committee behind the Lantern Tide Festival meets weekly through the winter to plan the programme.

## The programme

Every March, the Lantern Tide Festival takes over the streets of Port Ambrel. On the final evening a procession winds its way from the chapel down to the quay. Visitors are encouraged to join in the dancing, however clumsy their steps. Brass bands, fiddlers and choirs take turns on the small stage by the harbour wall. Families who have moved away often return specially for the Lantern Tide Festival. Sudden hail can roll in from the west with very little warning. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each spring.

Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Orrinhal is a windswept island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Celebrations at the Lantern Tide Festival last 7 days. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. The harbour at Lowcairn is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. In spring the moorland above Lowcairn turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Most houses in Lowcairn are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate.

## Who takes part

Organisers counted 84,306 visitors at the Lantern Tide Festival last year. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Port Ambrel by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. People in Lowcairn are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Many households combine several trades, such as dry-stone walling in winter and fishing in summer. Volunteers from Lowcairn give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair.

There is a strong tradition of boatbuilding, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Fenwick Quarwind, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. A noticeboard outside the shop in Lowcairn lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals.

## Setting

The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. The light on Orrinhal changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. From the high ground above Lowcairn the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day.

A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Lowcairn. A popular local saying holds that Orrinhal has four seasons in a single afternoon. Visiting writers have described it as salt-stained, a word the islanders have happily adopted. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. In Lowcairn it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea.

## Practical information

Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. There is a small café nearby that serves seed buns and strong tea. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out.

Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. No visit to the Lantern Tide Festival is complete without honeyed barley cakes, its traditional dish. Accommodation on Orrinhal is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths.

## Traditions

Banners at the Lantern Tide Festival carry its emblem, a leaping hare. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Port Ambrel and the neighbouring parishes. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. There is even a recipe for kelp broth that is said to have been named after it.
