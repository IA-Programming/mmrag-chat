# Quellmar Instruments

_A profile of Quellmar Instruments, a manufacturer based on Quillmere in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the company still stands out. The story of the company is tangled up with the story of Ashcombe itself. Staff often describe the company culture as practical, frugal and a little stubborn. Anyone hoping to understand Quillmere will sooner or later have to learn about the company. Travellers who arrive in Ashcombe usually hear about the company before they have even unpacked. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Ashcombe. Over the years the company has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago.

Local guidebooks tend to give the company only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Customers on the mainland have only recently begun to notice its products. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. This guide collects what is known about Quellmar Instruments and its place in the life of Quillmere. Much of the material here was gathered during long summer evenings in the reading room at Ashcombe. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred.

## Beginnings

Quellmar Instruments was founded in 1929 and moved into its present premises soon afterwards. Historians on Quillmere have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. Apprentices from the island school regularly join Quellmar Instruments after finishing their training. Quellmar Instruments began as a small workshop and still keeps a workbench from those early days. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. Several families from Tollhaven claim an ancestor who played a part in those years.

A stubborn letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. The founder of Quellmar Instruments, Tobin Dunholt, started the business in a converted boathouse. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. Most of the components are still assembled by hand in the main hall. The company sponsors the local football team and the annual regatta. Managers at Quellmar Instruments insist that quality matters more than speed.

## Products

A small showroom near the harbour displays early prototypes alongside the current range. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Gales can roll in from the west with very little warning. The light on Quillmere changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time.

Last year Quellmar Instruments reported revenue of 79.7 million Saltmere crowns. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. From the high ground above Tollhaven the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each spring.

## People

Quellmar Instruments employs 1551 people across its workshops and offices. Conversations in Ashcombe often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of smoked fish. People in Ashcombe are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. A noticeboard outside the shop in Ashcombe lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first summer on the island.

Caspian Belvisk, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. There is a strong tradition of basketry, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Many households combine several trades, such as knitting in winter and fishing in summer. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return.

## Home town

The harbour at Tollhaven is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Most houses in Ashcombe are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. The headquarters of Quellmar Instruments stand in Ashcombe, close to the harbour. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. In autumn the moorland above Ashcombe turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Quillmere is a patient island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

## Visiting the works

Accommodation on Quillmere is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out.

A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. There is a small café nearby that serves oatcakes and strong tea. The walk from the harbour at Ashcombe is pleasant but steep in places. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening.

## Notes

Several folk songs from Ashcombe mention it, usually in the last verse. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. A popular local saying holds that Quillmere has four seasons in a single afternoon. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Ashcombe. In Ashcombe it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea.

Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Ashcombe and the neighbouring parishes. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn. There is even a recipe for plum cake that is said to have been named after it. Visiting writers have described it as windswept, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects.
