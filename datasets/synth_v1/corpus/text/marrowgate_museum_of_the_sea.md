# Marrowgate Museum of the Sea

_A visitor's guide to the Marrowgate Museum of the Sea on Vellhaven, Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Much of the material here was gathered during long autumn evenings in the reading room at Marrowgate. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Calloway. Local guidebooks tend to give the museum only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. It is fair to say that the museum has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Marrowgate.

Anyone hoping to understand Vellhaven will sooner or later have to learn about the museum. Over the years the museum has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. The reading room of the Marrowgate Museum of the Sea is open to researchers by appointment. The shop sells postcards, local honey and books by island authors. Islanders tend to describe the museum with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Opinions about the museum differ, but nobody on Vellhaven is indifferent to it.

## Foundation

Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. It was in 1893 that the Marrowgate Museum of the Sea first opened to the public. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough.

## The collection

A volunteer team spends the winter cleaning, cataloguing and repairing objects. Some objects were donated by families who found them in attics and barns. The Marrowgate Museum of the Sea displays its collection across 16 rooms. School groups visit the Marrowgate Museum of the Sea regularly, and the education room is rarely empty. Labels are written in a friendly, unhurried style that suits the building. The galleries of the Marrowgate Museum of the Sea are arranged around a small courtyard planted with herbs. Temporary exhibitions change each spring and often focus on a single craft. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each winter.

A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Visitors to the Marrowgate Museum of the Sea usually head straight for a whale-bone sextant, its most famous object. Vellhaven is a stubborn island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. In winter the moorland above Marrowgate turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. The harbour at Marrowgate is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away.

The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. The Marrowgate Museum of the Sea holds 2646 objects in its collection. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Sudden hail can roll in from the west with very little warning. Most houses in Calloway are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish.

## Staff and volunteers

Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. The Marrowgate Museum of the Sea welcomes about 29,781 visitors a year. There is a strong tradition of net mending, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Jessamy Nethisk, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip.

A noticeboard outside the shop in Calloway lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. Conversations in Calloway often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of smoked fish. The curator of the Marrowgate Museum of the Sea is Zeno Pellaby. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Saul Vantvane, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first spring on the island. Volunteers from Marrowgate give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair.

## The building and its setting

The light on Vellhaven changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. From the high ground above Calloway the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. A popular local saying holds that Vellhaven has four seasons in a single afternoon. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Marrowgate. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining.

Visiting writers have described it as windswept, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. There is even a recipe for mutton stew that is said to have been named after it.

## Visiting

There is a small café nearby that serves kelp broth and strong tea. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Calloway on most mornings in autumn. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. The walk from the harbour at Marrowgate is pleasant but steep in places.

Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Accommodation on Vellhaven is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth.

## Notes

Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. In Calloway it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every summer. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Calloway and the neighbouring parishes. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel.
