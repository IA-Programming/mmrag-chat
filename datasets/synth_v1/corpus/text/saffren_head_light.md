# Saffren Head Light

_A guide to the Saffren Head Light, the lighthouse of Saffren in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Saffren as the lighthouse. The fog signal is sounded only rarely these days, but the islanders still remember its voice. Fishing crews heading home use the Saffren Head Light as a reassurance as much as a guide. Travellers who arrive in Pellby usually hear about the lighthouse before they have even unpacked. The lamp room of the Saffren Head Light is reached through a narrow trapdoor that tests the knees of every visitor.

The story of the lighthouse is tangled up with the story of Saffren Cross itself. Much of the material here was gathered during long autumn evenings in the reading room at Saffren Cross. Inside, the walls are lined with painted boards recording past keepers. On stormy nights the beam sweeps across the water in a slow, steady rhythm. Salt spray has worn the lower courses of stone into soft, rounded shapes. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Pellby.

## History of the light

Historians on Saffren have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. The early accounts mention a rugged wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. Several families from Pellby claim an ancestor who played a part in those years.

The great glass lens of the Saffren Head Light was made by the lens grinder Osric Dravmere. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. Older residents of Pellby still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. The keepers' cottages at the foot of the tower now house a small exhibition.

## The tower

The Saffren Head Light stands 21.9 m tall from its foundation stone to the tip of its weather vane. A brass logbook in the watch room of the Saffren Head Light records every ship that has passed within sight. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each summer.

From the high ground above Pellby the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. The light on Saffren changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. In autumn the moorland above Saffren Cross turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather.

## Keepers and neighbours

Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first spring on the island. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. There is a strong tradition of boatbuilding, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate.

Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Volunteers from Saffren Cross give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair. Isidore Correl, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. People in Saffren Cross are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. A noticeboard outside the shop in Saffren Cross lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. Many households combine several trades, such as stone carving in winter and fishing in summer.

## The coast around Saffren

The harbour at Saffren Cross is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. On a clear night the beam of the Saffren Head Light can be seen from 26 nautical miles away. Saffren is a proud island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Low cloud can roll in from the west with very little warning. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Most houses in Pellby are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves.

Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. In Saffren Cross it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Pellby and the neighbouring parishes. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Pellby.

## Visiting

The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. There is a small café nearby that serves crab soup and strong tea.

## Stories and notes

The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn. A popular local saying holds that Saffren has four seasons in a single afternoon. Several folk songs from Pellby mention it, usually in the last verse. Visiting writers have described it as weathered, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. There is even a recipe for mutton stew that is said to have been named after it.

The light of the Saffren Head Light flashes once every 6 seconds, a signature that fishing crews learn by heart. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. It is fair to say that the lighthouse has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Saffren Cross. Local guidebooks tend to give the lighthouse only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling.
