# Quillmere Clock Museum (archived 2019 edition)

_A visitor's guide to the Quillmere Clock Museum on Quillmere, Saltmere Archipelago._

_Archived 2019 edition, kept for reference only. Superseded by the revised 2024 edition._

## Overview

Islanders tend to describe the museum with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. It is fair to say that the museum has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Tollhaven. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Temporary exhibitions change each summer and often focus on a single craft. The story of the museum is tangled up with the story of Tollhaven itself. Over the years the museum has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago.

Opinions about the museum differ, but nobody on Quillmere is indifferent to it. Travellers who arrive in Tollhaven usually hear about the museum before they have even unpacked. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the museum still stands out. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Local guidebooks tend to give the museum only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice.

## Foundation

In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. A small exhibition in Ashcombe now displays photographs and tools from the period. The early accounts mention a weathered wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. Several families from Ashcombe claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. A sheltered letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. The parish ledgers of Quillmere record a long argument over who should pay for the work.

## The collection

Labels are written in a friendly, unhurried style that suits the building. The shop sells postcards, local honey and books by island authors. Some objects were donated by families who found them in attics and barns. A volunteer team spends the winter cleaning, cataloguing and repairing objects. School groups visit the Quillmere Clock Museum regularly, and the education room is rarely empty. The reading room of the Quillmere Clock Museum is open to researchers by appointment. The galleries of the Quillmere Clock Museum are arranged around a small courtyard planted with herbs.

From the high ground above Ashcombe the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. In winter the moorland above Ashcombe turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather.

## Staff and volunteers

Conversations in Tollhaven often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of kelp broth. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first spring on the island. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. A noticeboard outside the shop in Ashcombe lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Ashcombe by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. There is a strong tradition of weaving, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table.

Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Volunteers from Ashcombe give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. People in Ashcombe are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first.

## The building and its setting

The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. The harbour at Tollhaven is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Quillmere is a salt-stained island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. The light on Quillmere changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Most houses in Ashcombe are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Squalls can roll in from the west with very little warning.

## Visiting

Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Adults pay 5.50 Saltmere crowns to enter the Quillmere Clock Museum. (Figure as printed in the archived 2019 edition.) Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. The walk from the harbour at Tollhaven is pleasant but steep in places. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Accommodation on Quillmere is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season.

Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Tollhaven on most mornings in spring.

## Notes

Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Ashcombe. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day.

Several folk songs from Tollhaven mention it, usually in the last verse. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Ashcombe and the neighbouring parishes. A popular local saying holds that Quillmere has four seasons in a single afternoon. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. In Tollhaven it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea.
