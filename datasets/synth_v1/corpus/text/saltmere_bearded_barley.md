# Saltmere bearded barley

_Notes on Saltmere bearded barley, a crop grown on Brackenholt in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Much of the material here was gathered during long summer evenings in the reading room at Crowle Heath. Opinions about the crop differ, but nobody on Brackenholt is indifferent to it. Local guidebooks tend to give the crop only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Brackenholt as the crop.

The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Crowle Heath. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. This guide collects what is known about Saltmere bearded barley and its place in the life of Brackenholt. Some farmers swear by seaweed fertiliser, others by plain stubbornness.

## History of cultivation

A small exhibition in Wenmouth now displays photographs and tools from the period. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. The early accounts mention a windswept wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Old varieties of Saltmere bearded barley are kept alive in a small seed bank run by volunteers. A stubborn letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope.

Several families from Wenmouth claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Surplus Saltmere bearded barley is pressed, dried or turned into preserves for the winter. Cooks across the archipelago have their own favourite way of preparing Saltmere bearded barley. The parish ledgers of Brackenholt record a long argument over who should pay for the work.

## Growing

Market stalls in Wenmouth are piled high with Saltmere bearded barley when the season arrives. Saltmere bearded barley contains about 22% sugar by weight. Agronomists from the mainland have studied the crop with growing interest. The harvest is still largely done by hand, with whole families joining in. Growers on Brackenholt protect young Saltmere bearded barley plants from the wind with low stone walls. The light on Brackenholt changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn.

Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. When ripe, Saltmere bearded barley takes on a pale gold colour. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. From the high ground above Wenmouth the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Sea fog can roll in from the west with very little warning. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm.

In summer the moorland above Crowle Heath turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Growers bring in the Saltmere bearded barley harvest in January. Most houses in Crowle Heath are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Brackenholt is a proud island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn.

## Growers

The last harvest of Saltmere bearded barley came to 4320 tonnes. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Many households combine several trades, such as dry-stone walling in winter and fishing in summer. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Crowle Heath by bus or, in bad weather, not at all.

## Where it grows

The harbour at Wenmouth is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Visiting writers have described it as quiet, a word the islanders have happily adopted. In Crowle Heath it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves.

Most of the Saltmere bearded barley crop comes from Brackenholt. There is even a recipe for barley bread that is said to have been named after it. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Crowle Heath. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Wenmouth and the neighbouring parishes. Several folk songs from Wenmouth mention it, usually in the last verse. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining.

## Buying and eating

The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. The walk from the harbour at Crowle Heath is pleasant but steep in places. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay.

Expect to pay around 11.25 Saltmere crowns per kilogram for Saltmere bearded barley. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Accommodation on Brackenholt is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Wenmouth on most mornings in summer.

## Notes

Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn. A popular local saying holds that Brackenholt has four seasons in a single afternoon. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel.

It is fair to say that the crop has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Wenmouth. Anyone hoping to understand Brackenholt will sooner or later have to learn about the crop. Islanders tend to describe the crop with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. The story of the crop is tangled up with the story of Wenmouth itself. Travellers who arrive in Crowle Heath usually hear about the crop before they have even unpacked. Over the years the crop has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago.
