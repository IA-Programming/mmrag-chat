# Saffren Valley Line (revised 2024 edition)

_A guide to the Saffren Valley Line, a heritage railway on Saffren in the Saltmere Archipelago._

_Revised 2024 edition. This edition supersedes the archived 2019 edition._

## Overview

A small museum at the terminus displays signals, lamps and timetables. The carriages of the Saffren Valley Line are painted in a livery that has barely changed in living memory. It is fair to say that the railway has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Pellby. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Saffren Cross. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. The story of the railway is tangled up with the story of Saffren Cross itself.

Anyone hoping to understand Saffren will sooner or later have to learn about the railway. Opinions about the railway differ, but nobody on Saffren is indifferent to it. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Saffren as the railway. The line was built to carry stone and fish, and passengers were an afterthought. This guide collects what is known about the Saffren Valley Line and its place in the life of Saffren.

## Building the line

A modest letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. Older residents of Saffren Cross still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. Passenger services on the Saffren Valley Line began in 1906. Volunteers maintain the track, the stations and the small engine shed. Passengers often lean out of the windows to enjoy the views over the bay. Sheep on the line are a regular cause of short delays. Several families from Pellby claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns.

Historians on Saffren have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. The Saffren Valley Line was designed by the engineer Zeno Graskmarrow. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. Steam excursions on the Saffren Valley Line run on selected weekends and are booked out quickly. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride.

## Engines and carriages

The ticket office still issues old-fashioned card tickets. The harbour at Saffren Cross is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Low cloud can roll in from the west with very little warning. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn.

The light on Saffren changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. The top speed on the Saffren Valley Line is 68 km/h. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. In autumn the moorland above Pellby turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Saffren is a rugged island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Most houses in Saffren Cross are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish.

## Staff and volunteers

Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. There is a strong tradition of stone carving, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Saffren Cross by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. Sabine Quellisk, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. People in Pellby are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first.

Many households combine several trades, such as dry-stone walling in winter and fishing in summer. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first autumn on the island. Conversations in Saffren Cross often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of smoked fish.

## The route

From the high ground above Pellby the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Visiting writers have described it as unhurried, a word the islanders have happily adopted.

## Riding the line

Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill.

Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Accommodation on Saffren is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. There is a small café nearby that serves kelp broth and strong tea. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. The walk from the harbour at Saffren Cross is pleasant but steep in places. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards.

## Notes

In Saffren Cross it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea.

Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Several folk songs from Saffren Cross mention it, usually in the last verse. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves.
