# Kestrelmoor Light

_A guide to the Kestrelmoor Light, the lighthouse of Kestrelmoor in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Opinions about the lighthouse differ, but nobody on Kestrelmoor is indifferent to it. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Kestrelmoor as the lighthouse. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Hollinmere. Local guidebooks tend to give the lighthouse only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice.

It is fair to say that the lighthouse has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Hollinmere. Islanders tend to describe the lighthouse with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Travellers who arrive in Gullstead usually hear about the lighthouse before they have even unpacked. Salt spray has worn the lower courses of stone into soft, rounded shapes. This guide collects what is known about the Kestrelmoor Light and its place in the life of Kestrelmoor. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling.

## History of the light

In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. The parish ledgers of Kestrelmoor record a long argument over who should pay for the work. A brass logbook in the watch room of the Kestrelmoor Light records every ship that has passed within sight. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. A stubborn letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope.

The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. The fog signal is sounded only rarely these days, but the islanders still remember its voice. The lamp of the Kestrelmoor Light was first lit in 1847, after three winters of construction. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Historians on Kestrelmoor have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. The lamp room of the Kestrelmoor Light is reached through a narrow trapdoor that tests the knees of every visitor. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt.

## The tower

A spiral staircase of 308 steps leads from the entrance hall to the lamp room of the Kestrelmoor Light. On stormy nights the beam sweeps across the water in a slow, steady rhythm. The keepers' cottages at the foot of the tower now house a small exhibition. Inside, the walls are lined with painted boards recording past keepers. Fishing crews heading home use the Kestrelmoor Light as a reassurance as much as a guide. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn. The harbour at Hollinmere is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations.

The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Measured from foundation stone to weather vane, the Kestrelmoor Light rises 44.2 m. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. The light on Kestrelmoor changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish.

## Keepers and neighbours

Mira Branvane, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. People in Gullstead are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. Conversations in Gullstead often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of crab soup. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return.

There is a strong tradition of stone carving, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. The present keeper of the Kestrelmoor Light is Delphine Yarrowby, who took over the post after a long apprenticeship on the mainland. Talia Fennstead, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Many households combine several trades, such as knitting in winter and fishing in summer. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first summer on the island.

## The coast around Kestrelmoor

From the high ground above Hollinmere the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Low cloud can roll in from the west with very little warning. Most houses in Gullstead are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. In autumn the moorland above Hollinmere turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops.

## Visiting

There is a small café nearby that serves seed buns and strong tea. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay.

Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. The walk from the harbour at Hollinmere is pleasant but steep in places. Accommodation on Kestrelmoor is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season.

## Stories and notes

It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Hollinmere and the neighbouring parishes. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Fishing crews know the Kestrelmoor Light by its single flash every 10 seconds. In Gullstead it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Hollinmere. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects.

Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. There is even a recipe for barley bread that is said to have been named after it. A popular local saying holds that Kestrelmoor has four seasons in a single afternoon. Visiting writers have described it as patient, a word the islanders have happily adopted.
