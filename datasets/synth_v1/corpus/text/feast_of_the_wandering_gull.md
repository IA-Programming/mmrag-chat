# Feast of the Wandering Gull

_A guide to the Feast of the Wandering Gull, held on Halvric in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Opinions about the festival differ, but nobody on Halvric is indifferent to it. Anyone hoping to understand Halvric will sooner or later have to learn about the festival. On the final evening a procession winds its way from the chapel down to the quay. Islanders tend to describe the festival with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. The story of the festival is tangled up with the story of Halvric Bay itself.

The committee behind the Feast of the Wandering Gull meets weekly through the winter to plan the programme. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Halvric as the festival. Preparations for the Feast of the Wandering Gull begin months in advance in sheds and kitchens across the island. Brass bands, fiddlers and choirs take turns on the small stage by the harbour wall.

## Origins

The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. The early accounts mention a salt-stained wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. Stalls sell hot food, hand-made toys and souvenirs of every description. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Several families from Halvric Bay claim an ancestor who played a part in those years.

A small exhibition in Halvric Bay now displays photographs and tools from the period. Rain rarely stops the celebrations, although it sometimes shortens the speeches. The Feast of the Wandering Gull was held for the first time in 1905. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Older residents of Stoneacre still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. Historians on Halvric have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs.

## The programme

Families who have moved away often return specially for the Feast of the Wandering Gull. Visitors are encouraged to join in the dancing, however clumsy their steps. The harbour at Halvric Bay is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. In spring the moorland above Halvric Bay turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan.

Every August, the Feast of the Wandering Gull takes over the streets of Halvric Bay. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn. From the high ground above Halvric Bay the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. The light on Halvric changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Most houses in Stoneacre are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Sudden hail can roll in from the west with very little warning.

## Who takes part

Emrys Nethrey, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Vesper Varnstead, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. There is a strong tradition of dry-stone walling, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Volunteers from Stoneacre give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair.

Organisers counted 48,342 visitors at the Feast of the Wandering Gull last year. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first autumn on the island. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. People in Stoneacre are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Stoneacre by bus or, in bad weather, not at all.

## Setting

Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Halvric is a remote island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it.

Several folk songs from Stoneacre mention it, usually in the last verse. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. In Stoneacre it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Halvric Bay. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day.

## Practical information

Accommodation on Halvric is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. There is a small café nearby that serves seed buns and strong tea. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards.

Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Stoneacre on most mornings in summer. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay.

## Traditions

There is even a recipe for oatcakes that is said to have been named after it. Banners at the Feast of the Wandering Gull carry its emblem, a crossed oars and an anchor. A popular local saying holds that Halvric has four seasons in a single afternoon. Visiting writers have described it as quiet, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Halvric Bay and the neighbouring parishes. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects.

Local guidebooks tend to give the festival only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the festival still stands out. Travellers who arrive in Stoneacre usually hear about the festival before they have even unpacked. Much of the material here was gathered during long autumn evenings in the reading room at Halvric Bay. This guide collects what is known about the Feast of the Wandering Gull and its place in the life of Halvric. Over the years the festival has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago.
