# Corvane moth-hawk

_A field guide entry for the Corvane moth-hawk, a bird found on Corvane in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the bird still stands out. Opinions about the bird differ, but nobody on Corvane is indifferent to it. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. The story of the bird is tangled up with the story of Corvane Quay itself. Anyone hoping to understand Corvane will sooner or later have to learn about the bird. Local guidebooks tend to give the bird only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice.

Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Much of the material here was gathered during long spring evenings in the reading room at Dunlin. The bird is protected, and disturbing its nesting sites is an offence. This guide collects what is known about the Corvane moth-hawk and its place in the life of Corvane. Over the years the bird has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago.

## Discovery

The naturalist Isidore Ashmwind was the first to describe the Corvane moth-hawk. Several families from Dunlin claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. Photographers often wait for hours in hides to capture a good picture of it. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. The parish ledgers of Corvane record a long argument over who should pay for the work. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night.

Historians on Corvane have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. The early accounts mention a quiet wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. Naturalists on Corvane have kept careful notes on its behaviour for generations. A quiet letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. A small exhibition in Dunlin now displays photographs and tools from the period.

## Appearance and habits

Conservation volunteers count the Corvane moth-hawk each winter along standard survey routes. In the wild, the Corvane moth-hawk lives as long as 8 years. Gulls and stoats are a constant threat to its young. Children on the island learn to recognise the Corvane moth-hawk on school nature walks. The Corvane moth-hawk is shy, and patient observers are rewarded more often than noisy ones. Its call is often heard at dusk, carrying far across the water. Most houses in Corvane Quay are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Corvane is a patient island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

The wings of the Corvane moth-hawk span about 112 cm when fully spread. The light on Corvane changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. In spring the moorland above Corvane Quay turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Squalls can roll in from the west with very little warning. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms.

An adult Corvane moth-hawk measures about 81 cm from beak to tail. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. From the high ground above Corvane Quay the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. The harbour at Dunlin is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each spring.

## Watchers and researchers

Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Many households combine several trades, such as knitting in winter and fishing in summer. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Dunlin by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first spring on the island. People in Dunlin are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable.

Conversations in Dunlin often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of crab soup. Darrow Ashmigane, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. The last survey counted 3734 breeding pairs of the Corvane moth-hawk. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Volunteers from Dunlin give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair. Rafe Vantstead, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends.

## Habitat

The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every summer. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Most pairs of the Corvane moth-hawk nest in old stone walls on the moorland. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. A popular local saying holds that Corvane has four seasons in a single afternoon. In Corvane Quay it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Dunlin.

Visiting writers have described it as unhurried, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Several folk songs from Corvane Quay mention it, usually in the last verse. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel.

## Watching it

Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Accommodation on Corvane is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season.

Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. The walk from the harbour at Dunlin is pleasant but steep in places.

## Folklore

It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Dunlin and the neighbouring parishes. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. There is even a recipe for barley bread that is said to have been named after it. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves.

Travellers who arrive in Corvane Quay usually hear about the bird before they have even unpacked. Islanders tend to describe the bird with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation.
