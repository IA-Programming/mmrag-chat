# Brackenholt Sky Station

_A guide to the Brackenholt Sky Station, the observatory on Brackenholt in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

The Brackenholt Sky Station keeps a strict rule against bright lights on the access road after dusk. Clear, dark skies are the main reason the site was chosen. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Crowle Heath. Local guidebooks tend to give the observatory only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. The dome opens with a long metallic groan that can be heard far down the valley.

It is fair to say that the observatory has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Wenmouth. Anyone hoping to understand Brackenholt will sooner or later have to learn about the observatory. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Brackenholt as the observatory. Opinions about the observatory differ, but nobody on Brackenholt is indifferent to it. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Islanders tend to describe the observatory with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. This guide collects what is known about the Brackenholt Sky Station and its place in the life of Brackenholt.

## Origins

The main mirror of the Brackenholt Sky Station was installed in 1956, after a long journey by sea. The early accounts mention a stubborn wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. A proud letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. Older residents of Wenmouth still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days.

The parish ledgers of Brackenholt record a long argument over who should pay for the work. A small library at the Brackenholt Sky Station holds star atlases, logbooks and hand-drawn charts of the night sky. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. Historians on Brackenholt have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs.

## Instruments

Students from across the archipelago compete for a few nights of observing time each year. Public open nights are popular, especially when a bright planet is well placed. A 2.8 m mirror, ground from one blank of glass, sits at the heart of the main telescope of the Brackenholt Sky Station. Astronomers at the Brackenholt Sky Station share their data with partner institutes on the mainland. The control room smells faintly of coffee, machine oil and old paper. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. The light on Brackenholt changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time.

Gales can roll in from the west with very little warning. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. From the high ground above Wenmouth the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each spring. The harbour at Crowle Heath is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away.

## The site

Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Brackenholt is a modest island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Most houses in Crowle Heath are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate.

Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. In summer the moorland above Crowle Heath turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. At 1752 m above sea level, the Brackenholt Sky Station sits above the island's cloud layer on most nights. Several folk songs from Crowle Heath mention it, usually in the last verse. A popular local saying holds that Brackenholt has four seasons in a single afternoon. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name.

## Staff and students

Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first autumn on the island. Nell Elmrington directs the Brackenholt Sky Station and still spends several nights a month at the eyepiece. Elspeth Lusraith, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Odette Ombwake, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends.

Many households combine several trades, such as weaving in winter and fishing in summer. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Wenmouth by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. There is a strong tradition of boatbuilding, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return.

## Open nights

Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Accommodation on Brackenholt is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain.

Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Wenmouth on most mornings in winter. There is a small café nearby that serves smoked fish and strong tea. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay.

## Miscellany

There is even a recipe for plum cake that is said to have been named after it. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every summer. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Visiting writers have described it as modest, a word the islanders have happily adopted. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Wenmouth and the neighbouring parishes.

Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day.
