# Glimmerfruit

_Notes on Glimmerfruit, a crop grown on Orrinhal in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Much of the material here was gathered during long autumn evenings in the reading room at Port Ambrel. Market stalls in Port Ambrel are piled high with Glimmerfruit when the season arrives. Opinions about the crop differ, but nobody on Orrinhal is indifferent to it. Cooks across the archipelago have their own favourite way of preparing Glimmerfruit. Local guidebooks tend to give the crop only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Some farmers swear by seaweed fertiliser, others by plain stubbornness. Over the years the crop has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago.

Agronomists from the mainland have studied the crop with growing interest. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Anyone hoping to understand Orrinhal will sooner or later have to learn about the crop. Growers on Orrinhal protect young Glimmerfruit plants from the wind with low stone walls. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the crop still stands out. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. This guide collects what is known about Glimmerfruit and its place in the life of Orrinhal.

## History of cultivation

Older residents of Lowcairn still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. The early accounts mention a unhurried wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. Glimmerfruit was first cultivated by Perrin Fennraith, a farmer from Port Ambrel. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Historians on Orrinhal have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night.

## Growing

The harvest is still largely done by hand, with whole families joining in. Growers bring in the Glimmerfruit harvest in September. Old varieties of Glimmerfruit are kept alive in a small seed bank run by volunteers. Surplus Glimmerfruit is pressed, dried or turned into preserves for the winter. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Orrinhal is a rugged island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Most houses in Port Ambrel are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan.

The light on Orrinhal changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Ripe Glimmerfruit turns a burnished amber colour. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. From the high ground above Lowcairn the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away.

## Growers

Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first summer on the island. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. Last season growers harvested 2828 tonnes of Glimmerfruit. Conversations in Port Ambrel often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of smoked fish. People in Port Ambrel are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Lowcairn by bus or, in bad weather, not at all.

## Where it grows

In autumn the moorland above Lowcairn turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. The harbour at Lowcairn is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Most of the Glimmerfruit crop comes from Orrinhal. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Squalls can roll in from the west with very little warning. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn.

A popular local saying holds that Orrinhal has four seasons in a single afternoon. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Several folk songs from Port Ambrel mention it, usually in the last verse. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects.

## Buying and eating

Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Accommodation on Orrinhal is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. The walk from the harbour at Lowcairn is pleasant but steep in places. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says.

## Notes

A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Port Ambrel. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. There is even a recipe for seed buns that is said to have been named after it. In Lowcairn it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day.

Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Port Ambrel and the neighbouring parishes. Visiting writers have described it as weathered, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Islanders tend to describe the crop with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. It is fair to say that the crop has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Lowcairn. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date.
