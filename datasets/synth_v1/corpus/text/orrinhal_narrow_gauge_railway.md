# Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway

_A guide to the Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway, a heritage railway on Orrinhal in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

The ticket office still issues old-fashioned card tickets. The carriages of the Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway are painted in a livery that has barely changed in living memory. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Orrinhal as the railway. Travellers who arrive in Port Ambrel usually hear about the railway before they have even unpacked. A small museum at the terminus displays signals, lamps and timetables. The story of the railway is tangled up with the story of Port Ambrel itself.

Opinions about the railway differ, but nobody on Orrinhal is indifferent to it. Steam excursions on the Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway run on selected weekends and are booked out quickly. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. This guide collects what is known about the Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway and its place in the life of Orrinhal. Sheep on the line are a regular cause of short delays.

## Building the line

Passengers often lean out of the windows to enjoy the views over the bay. The engineer Mira Undeell drew up the plans for the Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. A windswept letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Older residents of Port Ambrel still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. The line was built to carry stone and fish, and passengers were an afterthought. Several families from Port Ambrel claim an ancestor who played a part in those years.

Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Passenger services on the Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway began in 1902. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. The early accounts mention a remote wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once.

## Engines and carriages

Volunteers maintain the track, the stations and the small engine shed. Orrinhal is a windswept island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. The oldest engine on the Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway is called Old Bess. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. The light on Orrinhal changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn.

Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Low cloud can roll in from the west with very little warning. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. From the high ground above Lowcairn the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. In autumn the moorland above Port Ambrel turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Most houses in Port Ambrel are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate.

## Staff and volunteers

Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Odette Lusaby, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Conversations in Port Ambrel often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of plum cake. Many households combine several trades, such as stone carving in winter and fishing in summer. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate.

## The route

The Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway serves 23 stations along its route. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn. The harbour at Port Ambrel is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Lowcairn.

The Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway runs for 57.3 km from end to end. In Port Ambrel it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. There is even a recipe for crab soup that is said to have been named after it. Several folk songs from Port Ambrel mention it, usually in the last verse. A popular local saying holds that Orrinhal has four seasons in a single afternoon. Visiting writers have described it as modest, a word the islanders have happily adopted.

## Riding the line

Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay.

Accommodation on Orrinhal is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. The Orrinhal Narrow-Gauge Railway charges 11.25 Saltmere crowns for a return ticket. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Lowcairn on most mornings in summer. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says.

## Notes

The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every summer. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Port Ambrel and the neighbouring parishes. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it.

Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Over the years the railway has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. Islanders tend to describe the railway with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation.
