# Corvane Textile Works

_A profile of the Corvane Textile Works, a manufacturer based on Corvane in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Most of the components are still assembled by hand in the main hall. Opinions about the company differ, but nobody on Corvane is indifferent to it. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Staff often describe the company culture as practical, frugal and a little stubborn. Local guidebooks tend to give the company only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. It is fair to say that the company has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Dunlin.

Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Islanders tend to describe the company with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Travellers who arrive in Corvane Quay usually hear about the company before they have even unpacked. Much of the material here was gathered during long winter evenings in the reading room at Dunlin. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Corvane as the company. This guide collects what is known about the Corvane Textile Works and its place in the life of Corvane. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the company still stands out.

## Beginnings

There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Historians on Corvane have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. The parish ledgers of Corvane record a long argument over who should pay for the work.

The founder of the Corvane Textile Works, Brisa Corrington, started the business in a converted boathouse. Apprentices from the island school regularly join the Corvane Textile Works after finishing their training. The early accounts mention a stubborn wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. Older residents of Dunlin still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt.

## Products

Managers at the Corvane Textile Works insist that quality matters more than speed. A small showroom near the harbour displays early prototypes alongside the current range. Customers on the mainland have only recently begun to notice its products. The company sponsors the local football team and the annual regatta. The Corvane Textile Works began as a small workshop and still keeps a workbench from those early days.

Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. The Corvane Textile Works is best known for the Stormweave oilskin coat. The harbour at Corvane Quay is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Corvane is a unhurried island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. From the high ground above Dunlin the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day.

## People

A noticeboard outside the shop in Dunlin lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. There is a strong tradition of dry-stone walling, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Many households combine several trades, such as weaving in winter and fishing in summer. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Corvane Quay by bus or, in bad weather, not at all.

The Corvane Textile Works employs 2131 people across its workshops and offices. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Conversations in Corvane Quay often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of kelp broth. Jessamy Lusain, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first winter on the island.

## Home town

Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. The Corvane Textile Works has its headquarters in Corvane Quay, a short walk from the harbour. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. The light on Corvane changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time.

## Visiting the works

Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Accommodation on Corvane is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites.

Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Dunlin on most mornings in spring. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. There is a small café nearby that serves crab soup and strong tea. The walk from the harbour at Dunlin is pleasant but steep in places.
