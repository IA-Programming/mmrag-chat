# Ysolde Ridge Observatory

_A guide to the Ysolde Ridge Observatory, the observatory on Ysolde in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Ysolde as the observatory. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Over the years the observatory has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. Public open nights are popular, especially when a bright planet is well placed. This guide collects what is known about the Ysolde Ridge Observatory and its place in the life of Ysolde.

Travellers who arrive in Ysolde Harbour usually hear about the observatory before they have even unpacked. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Islanders tend to describe the observatory with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. The story of the observatory is tangled up with the story of Merrow End itself. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred.

## Origins

The control room smells faintly of coffee, machine oil and old paper. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. The parish ledgers of Ysolde record a long argument over who should pay for the work. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. Several families from Merrow End claim an ancestor who played a part in those years.

The Ysolde Ridge Observatory keeps a strict rule against bright lights on the access road after dusk. It was in 2012 that the main mirror of the Ysolde Ridge Observatory was finally installed. A proud letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. Students from across the archipelago compete for a few nights of observing time each year. Astronomers at the Ysolde Ridge Observatory share their data with partner institutes on the mainland. A small exhibition in Ysolde Harbour now displays photographs and tools from the period.

## Instruments

The dome of the Ysolde Ridge Observatory, 14.8 m across, is visible from most of the island. The dome opens with a long metallic groan that can be heard far down the valley. Clear, dark skies are the main reason the site was chosen. A small library at the Ysolde Ridge Observatory holds star atlases, logbooks and hand-drawn charts of the night sky. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each winter. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm.

Staff at the Ysolde Ridge Observatory call their largest telescope the Old Heron. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Most houses in Ysolde Harbour are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Sea fog can roll in from the west with very little warning. Ysolde is a quiet island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. The light on Ysolde changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time.

Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. The main telescope of the Ysolde Ridge Observatory uses a 1.8 m mirror, ground from a single blank of glass. In summer the moorland above Merrow End turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away.

## The site

From the high ground above Ysolde Harbour the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. The Ysolde Ridge Observatory sits at an altitude of 2096 m above sea level. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. The harbour at Merrow End is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Ysolde Harbour. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining.

## Staff and students

Many households combine several trades, such as dry-stone walling in winter and fishing in summer. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Teodor Dravquist directs the Ysolde Ridge Observatory and still spends several nights a month at the eyepiece. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first spring on the island.

## Open nights

Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Ysolde Harbour on most mornings in autumn. The walk from the harbour at Ysolde Harbour is pleasant but steep in places.

Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay. There is a small café nearby that serves mutton stew and strong tea. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards.

## Miscellany

It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Ysolde Harbour and the neighbouring parishes. There is even a recipe for oatcakes that is said to have been named after it. In Ysolde Harbour it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every spring. Visiting writers have described it as patient, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop.

A popular local saying holds that Ysolde has four seasons in a single afternoon. Several folk songs from Merrow End mention it, usually in the last verse. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Much of the material here was gathered during long spring evenings in the reading room at Merrow End.
