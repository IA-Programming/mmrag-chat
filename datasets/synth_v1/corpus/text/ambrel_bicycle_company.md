# Ambrel Bicycle Company

_A profile of the Ambrel Bicycle Company, a manufacturer based on Thistlewane in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. A small showroom near the harbour displays early prototypes alongside the current range. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the company still stands out. Travellers who arrive in Thistle Hythe usually hear about the company before they have even unpacked. The story of the company is tangled up with the story of Larkspur itself.

Opinions about the company differ, but nobody on Thistlewane is indifferent to it. Apprentices from the island school regularly join the Ambrel Bicycle Company after finishing their training. Over the years the company has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Thistle Hythe. Local guidebooks tend to give the company only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice.

## Beginnings

The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. The founder of the Ambrel Bicycle Company, Faye Nethnell, started the business in a converted boathouse. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. Staff often describe the company culture as practical, frugal and a little stubborn. The company sponsors the local football team and the annual regatta. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. Older residents of Larkspur still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers.

Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Managers at the Ambrel Bicycle Company insist that quality matters more than speed. The Ambrel Bicycle Company was founded in 1963 and moved into its present premises soon afterwards. Several families from Thistle Hythe claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. Most of the components are still assembled by hand in the main hall. Historians on Thistlewane have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. The Ambrel Bicycle Company began as a small workshop and still keeps a workbench from those early days. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride.

## Products

Customers on the mainland have only recently begun to notice its products. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each summer. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away.

Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. From the high ground above Larkspur the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. In summer the moorland above Thistle Hythe turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. The harbour at Thistle Hythe is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Squalls can roll in from the west with very little warning.

## People

The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. The Ambrel Bicycle Company employs 1221 people across its workshops and offices. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Larkspur by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. There is a strong tradition of basketry, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Saul Fennoch, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Many households combine several trades, such as stone carving in winter and fishing in summer.

## Home town

Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. The Ambrel Bicycle Company has its headquarters in Larkspur, a short walk from the harbour. The light on Thistlewane changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Thistlewane is a remote island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Most houses in Larkspur are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop.

In Larkspur it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Several folk songs from Larkspur mention it, usually in the last verse. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. There is even a recipe for barley bread that is said to have been named after it. A popular local saying holds that Thistlewane has four seasons in a single afternoon.

## Visiting the works

Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Thistle Hythe on most mornings in spring. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office.

## Notes

The motto painted above the door of the Ambrel Bicycle Company reads "From the shore, for the shore". The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Thistle Hythe and the neighbouring parishes. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Larkspur. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Visiting writers have described it as remote, a word the islanders have happily adopted.

Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Thistlewane as the company. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Anyone hoping to understand Thistlewane will sooner or later have to learn about the company.
