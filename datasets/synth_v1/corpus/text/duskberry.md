# Duskberry

_Notes on Duskberry, a crop grown on Duskwater in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Travellers who arrive in Fernmouth usually hear about the crop before they have even unpacked. Over the years the crop has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. Market stalls in Eddersby are piled high with Duskberry when the season arrives. It is fair to say that the crop has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Eddersby. Some farmers swear by seaweed fertiliser, others by plain stubbornness.

Islanders tend to describe the crop with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Much of the material here was gathered during long winter evenings in the reading room at Eddersby. Opinions about the crop differ, but nobody on Duskwater is indifferent to it. The harvest is still largely done by hand, with whole families joining in. Surplus Duskberry is pressed, dried or turned into preserves for the winter.

## History of cultivation

There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Duskberry was first cultivated by Wren Talel, a farmer from Fernmouth. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Agronomists from the mainland have studied the crop with growing interest. A small exhibition in Eddersby now displays photographs and tools from the period. Growers on Duskwater protect young Duskberry plants from the wind with low stone walls. The early accounts mention a salt-stained wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once.

## Growing

Cooks across the archipelago have their own favourite way of preparing Duskberry. The Duskberry harvest takes place in November, when the crop is fully ripe. Old varieties of Duskberry are kept alive in a small seed bank run by volunteers. The light on Duskwater changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Duskwater is a remote island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each summer. Most houses in Eddersby are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. The harbour at Eddersby is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn.

## Growers

Last season growers harvested 8448 tonnes of Duskberry. Volunteers from Fernmouth give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Many households combine several trades, such as boatbuilding in winter and fishing in summer. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Fernmouth by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Quilla Correrby, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish.

Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first summer on the island. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. Conversations in Eddersby often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of mutton stew. People in Fernmouth are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. A noticeboard outside the shop in Fernmouth lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals.

## Where it grows

A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. From the high ground above Fernmouth the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Drizzle can roll in from the west with very little warning. In autumn the moorland above Eddersby turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. A popular local saying holds that Duskwater has four seasons in a single afternoon.

Duskberry is grown mainly on Duskwater. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Eddersby and the neighbouring parishes. There is even a recipe for smoked fish that is said to have been named after it.

## Buying and eating

Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. There is a small café nearby that serves crab soup and strong tea. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth.

## Notes

Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. In Eddersby it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every summer. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Fernmouth. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves.

Several folk songs from Fernmouth mention it, usually in the last verse. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Visiting writers have described it as salt-stained, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Local guidebooks tend to give the crop only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice.
