# Velmora Observatory

_A guide to the Velmora Observatory, the observatory on Vellhaven in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Over the years the observatory has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. The story of the observatory is tangled up with the story of Calloway itself. Travellers who arrive in Calloway usually hear about the observatory before they have even unpacked. Anyone hoping to understand Vellhaven will sooner or later have to learn about the observatory. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the observatory still stands out.

Islanders tend to describe the observatory with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. This guide collects what is known about the Velmora Observatory and its place in the life of Vellhaven. It is fair to say that the observatory has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Marrowgate. Local guidebooks tend to give the observatory only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice.

## Origins

Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. A salt-stained letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. The main mirror of the Velmora Observatory was installed in 2015, after a long journey by sea. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. Older residents of Marrowgate still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. Students from across the archipelago compete for a few nights of observing time each year. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride.

## Instruments

The dome of the Velmora Observatory, 10.9 m across, is visible from most of the island. The Velmora Observatory keeps a strict rule against bright lights on the access road after dusk. A small library at the Velmora Observatory holds star atlases, logbooks and hand-drawn charts of the night sky. Public open nights are popular, especially when a bright planet is well placed. The control room smells faintly of coffee, machine oil and old paper. Clear, dark skies are the main reason the site was chosen. The dome opens with a long metallic groan that can be heard far down the valley.

The main telescope of the Velmora Observatory uses a 2.4 m mirror, ground from a single blank of glass. Astronomers at the Velmora Observatory share their data with partner institutes on the mainland. The light on Vellhaven changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Most houses in Calloway are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm.

## The site

Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Records kept at the Velmora Observatory show an average of 169 clear nights each year. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. In summer the moorland above Marrowgate turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. The harbour at Marrowgate is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Low cloud can roll in from the west with very little warning. From the high ground above Marrowgate the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day.

At 1121 m above sea level, the Velmora Observatory sits above the island's cloud layer on most nights. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each summer. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Vellhaven is a salt-stained island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. Several folk songs from Calloway mention it, usually in the last verse. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining.

## Staff and students

Jessamy Rothvane, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Caspian Branel directs the Velmora Observatory and still spends several nights a month at the eyepiece. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. People in Marrowgate are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Ulric Fennworth, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Marrowgate by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. Volunteers from Marrowgate give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair.

Conversations in Calloway often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of oatcakes. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations. There is a strong tradition of net mending, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. Many households combine several trades, such as net mending in winter and fishing in summer. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first summer on the island. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate.

## Open nights

Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. The walk from the harbour at Marrowgate is pleasant but steep in places. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay.

## Miscellany

In Calloway it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every spring. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves.
