# Duskwater Kite Week

_A guide to Duskwater Kite Week, held on Duskwater in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Travellers who arrive in Eddersby usually hear about the festival before they have even unpacked. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the festival still stands out. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Eddersby. Anyone hoping to understand Duskwater will sooner or later have to learn about the festival. Over the years the festival has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. The story of the festival is tangled up with the story of Fernmouth itself.

Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Duskwater as the festival. Much of the material here was gathered during long winter evenings in the reading room at Eddersby. Opinions about the festival differ, but nobody on Duskwater is indifferent to it. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. Local guidebooks tend to give the festival only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice.

## Origins

Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Several families from Eddersby claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. Historians on Duskwater have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. Older residents of Eddersby still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. A small exhibition in Fernmouth now displays photographs and tools from the period.

The early accounts mention a rugged wooden shed that served as office, store and kitchen at once. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers. The parish ledgers of Duskwater record a long argument over who should pay for the work. A unhurried letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. Preparations for Duskwater Kite Week begin months in advance in sheds and kitchens across the island. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride.

## The programme

On the final evening a procession winds its way from the chapel down to the quay. Celebrations at Duskwater Kite Week last 8 days. Visitors are encouraged to join in the dancing, however clumsy their steps. Rain rarely stops the celebrations, although it sometimes shortens the speeches. Families who have moved away often return specially for Duskwater Kite Week. Stalls sell hot food, hand-made toys and souvenirs of every description. Brass bands, fiddlers and choirs take turns on the small stage by the harbour wall. The committee behind Duskwater Kite Week meets weekly through the winter to plan the programme.

Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Duskwater Kite Week is held every year in June. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Most houses in Fernmouth are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. The harbour at Fernmouth is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish.

## Who takes part

Last year Duskwater Kite Week drew 49,565 visitors. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Lorcan Vantquist, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Volunteers from Eddersby give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair. Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first winter on the island. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate.

Petra Belvel chairs the volunteer committee that organises Duskwater Kite Week. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. Kit Quellcott, a retired schoolteacher, has written a short history of the parish. Conversations in Eddersby often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of crab soup. Many households combine several trades, such as weaving in winter and fishing in summer. Island families tend to stay put, and the same surnames appear in the records for generations.

## Setting

The light on Duskwater changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Sudden hail can roll in from the west with very little warning. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each autumn. Duskwater is a quiet island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

## Practical information

No visit to Duskwater Kite Week is complete without treacle oat bannocks, its traditional dish. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Sturdy footwear is recommended, as the paths can be muddy after rain. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. Accommodation on Duskwater is limited, and rooms fill up quickly in the busy season.

There is a small café nearby that serves plum cake and strong tea. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Eddersby on most mornings in winter. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites.

## Traditions

Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. The emblem of Duskwater Kite Week is a leaping salmon. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Eddersby and the neighbouring parishes. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Fernmouth. A popular local saying holds that Duskwater has four seasons in a single afternoon. There is even a recipe for mutton stew that is said to have been named after it. In Fernmouth it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea.
