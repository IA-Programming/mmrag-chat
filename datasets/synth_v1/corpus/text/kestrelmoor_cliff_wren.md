# Kestrelmoor cliff wren

_A field guide entry for the Kestrelmoor cliff wren, a bird found on Kestrelmoor in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

It is fair to say that the bird has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Hollinmere. Children on the island learn to recognise the Kestrelmoor cliff wren on school nature walks. Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. The Kestrelmoor cliff wren is shy, and patient observers are rewarded more often than noisy ones. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. The story of the bird is tangled up with the story of Hollinmere itself.

Photographers often wait for hours in hides to capture a good picture of it. Anyone hoping to understand Kestrelmoor will sooner or later have to learn about the bird. Local guidebooks tend to give the bird only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. This guide collects what is known about the Kestrelmoor cliff wren and its place in the life of Kestrelmoor. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Kestrelmoor as the bird. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the bird still stands out. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date.

## Discovery

The archipelago was poorer then, and every project competed with the fishing fleet for hands. A quiet letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. The naturalist Fenwick Belvvane was the first to describe the Kestrelmoor cliff wren. Several families from Hollinmere claim an ancestor who played a part in those years. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers.

The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. The parish ledgers of Kestrelmoor record a long argument over who should pay for the work. Historians on Kestrelmoor have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs. Naturalists on Kestrelmoor have kept careful notes on its behaviour for generations. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns.

## Appearance and habits

Clutches of the Kestrelmoor cliff wren usually hold 2 eggs. Gulls and stoats are a constant threat to its young. Conservation volunteers count the Kestrelmoor cliff wren each autumn along standard survey routes. Its call is often heard at dusk, carrying far across the water. The bird is protected, and disturbing its nesting sites is an offence. The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Kestrelmoor is a stubborn island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

Most houses in Gullstead are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. A wild Kestrelmoor cliff wren can live for up to 10 years. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Squalls can roll in from the west with very little warning. The harbour at Hollinmere is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. In autumn the moorland above Gullstead turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. From the high ground above Hollinmere the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day.

From beak to tail, an adult Kestrelmoor cliff wren reaches about 17 cm. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each spring. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather.

## Watchers and researchers

Many households combine several trades, such as dry-stone walling in winter and fishing in summer. Surveyors recorded 6697 breeding pairs of the Kestrelmoor cliff wren at the last count. Conversations in Hollinmere often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of smoked fish. Volunteers from Hollinmere give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair. Wenna Caelnell, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable.

## Habitat

The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. The light on Kestrelmoor changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time. Most pairs of the Kestrelmoor cliff wren nest in dune slacks behind sandy beaches. Visiting writers have described it as weathered, a word the islanders have happily adopted. It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Hollinmere and the neighbouring parishes. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. Several folk songs from Hollinmere mention it, usually in the last verse.

Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Hollinmere. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. A popular local saying holds that Kestrelmoor has four seasons in a single afternoon. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. In Hollinmere it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea.

## Watching it

Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. Guided walks leave from the visitor hut in Gullstead on most mornings in summer. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office.

## Folklore

There is even a recipe for barley bread that is said to have been named after it. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every winter. Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it.

Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Over the years the bird has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. Opinions about the bird differ, but nobody on Kestrelmoor is indifferent to it. Travellers who arrive in Gullstead usually hear about the bird before they have even unpacked. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Hollinmere. Islanders tend to describe the bird with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Much of the material here was gathered during long winter evenings in the reading room at Hollinmere.
