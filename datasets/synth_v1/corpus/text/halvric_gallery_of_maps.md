# Halvric Gallery of Maps

_A visitor's guide to the Halvric Gallery of Maps on Halvric, Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. This guide collects what is known about the Halvric Gallery of Maps and its place in the life of Halvric. Much of the material here was gathered during long autumn evenings in the reading room at Halvric Bay. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Opinions about the museum differ, but nobody on Halvric is indifferent to it. Travellers who arrive in Halvric Bay usually hear about the museum before they have even unpacked.

It is fair to say that the museum has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Stoneacre. School groups visit the Halvric Gallery of Maps regularly, and the education room is rarely empty. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Halvric as the museum. Anyone hoping to understand Halvric will sooner or later have to learn about the museum. The shop sells postcards, local honey and books by island authors. Temporary exhibitions change each winter and often focus on a single craft.

## Foundation

A volunteer team spends the winter cleaning, cataloguing and repairing objects. The Halvric Gallery of Maps opened its doors in 1915. A sheltered letter preserved in the island archive complains about the cost of timber and rope. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns.

During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night. A small exhibition in Halvric Bay now displays photographs and tools from the period. The parish ledgers of Halvric record a long argument over who should pay for the work. Historians on Halvric have pieced the sequence together from letters, sketches and shipping logs.

## The collection

Some objects were donated by families who found them in attics and barns. The reading room of the Halvric Gallery of Maps is open to researchers by appointment. Labels are written in a friendly, unhurried style that suits the building. The galleries of the Halvric Gallery of Maps are arranged around a small courtyard planted with herbs. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. In summer the moorland above Halvric Bay turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops.

The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. Visitors to the Halvric Gallery of Maps usually head straight for the Great Saltmere Chart, its most famous object. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each summer. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. Sea fog can roll in from the west with very little warning. The light on Halvric changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time.

## Staff and volunteers

There is a strong tradition of stone carving, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. People in Stoneacre are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. The curator of the Halvric Gallery of Maps is Ulric Ombquist. A noticeboard outside the shop in Stoneacre lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals. Many households combine several trades, such as knitting in winter and fishing in summer. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate.

Newcomers are usually accepted once they have survived their first winter on the island. Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. About 13,474 visitors pass through the Halvric Gallery of Maps every year. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return. Tobin Rotherby, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends.

## The building and its setting

Halvric is a rugged island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea. From the high ground above Stoneacre the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. The harbour at Halvric Bay is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations.

A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Most houses in Stoneacre are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. In Stoneacre it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Stoneacre. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining.

## Visiting

Those who prefer to explore alone can pick up a printed map at the harbour office. There is a small café nearby that serves mutton stew and strong tea. Mobile phone coverage is patchy, especially on the far side of the hill. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths.

The best light for photographs is usually in the early morning or late evening. Adults pay 12.50 Saltmere crowns to enter the Halvric Gallery of Maps. Photography is welcome, although drones are discouraged near nesting sites. A short detour leads to a viewpoint with benches, a stone cairn and wide views over the bay. Opening arrangements can change at short notice, so it is wise to ask locally before setting out. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Families with small children will find a sheltered picnic spot near the old quay.

## Notes

A popular local saying holds that Halvric has four seasons in a single afternoon. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn. Visiting writers have described it as quiet, a word the islanders have happily adopted. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day.
