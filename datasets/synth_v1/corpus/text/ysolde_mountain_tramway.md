# Ysolde Mountain Tramway

_A guide to the Ysolde Mountain Tramway, a heritage railway on Ysolde in the Saltmere Archipelago._

## Overview

Readers new to the islands should note that the place names here follow local spelling. Anyone hoping to understand Ysolde will sooner or later have to learn about the railway. Islanders tend to describe the railway with a mixture of affection and mild exasperation. Local guidebooks tend to give the railway only a short paragraph, which hardly does it justice. Over the years the railway has become something of a symbol for the whole archipelago. Sheep on the line are a regular cause of short delays.

Travellers who arrive in Merrow End usually hear about the railway before they have even unpacked. Where sources disagree, the more cautious version has been preferred. Passengers often lean out of the windows to enjoy the views over the bay. The ticket office still issues old-fashioned card tickets. Few subjects come up as often in conversation on Ysolde as the railway. What follows draws on parish records, interviews and the notebooks of several long-time residents of Merrow End. Opinions about the railway differ, but nobody on Ysolde is indifferent to it.

## Building the line

Some details from those years survive only in songs that are still sung in the harbour taverns. Older residents of Ysolde Harbour still tell stories passed down from their grandparents about the early days. The engineer Caspian Orrhay drew up the plans for the Ysolde Mountain Tramway. The work attracted craftspeople from across the archipelago, many of whom stayed for good. Steam excursions on the Ysolde Mountain Tramway run on selected weekends and are booked out quickly. Later generations simplified the story, as later generations usually do. There were setbacks, including a storm that carried away half a season's work in a single night.

The Ysolde Mountain Tramway opened to passengers in 1931. In time the scepticism of the early years gave way to a cautious local pride. The line was built to carry stone and fish, and passengers were an afterthought. Newspapers on the mainland paid little attention, which suited the islanders well enough. The carriages of the Ysolde Mountain Tramway are painted in a livery that has barely changed in living memory. Volunteers maintain the track, the stations and the small engine shed. During the hard winters that followed, progress slowed almost to a halt. Records from that period are patchy, as a fire in the old customs house destroyed many papers.

## Engines and carriages

A small museum at the terminus displays signals, lamps and timetables. A single road loops around the island, with tracks branching off towards the farms. On the Ysolde Mountain Tramway the oldest engine goes by the name the Duchess of Pellby. The harbour at Ysolde Harbour is small but deep, and it has sheltered boats for generations. Seabirds nest in their thousands along the northern cliffs each winter. The light on Ysolde changes quickly, which has drawn painters to the island for a long time.

The soil is thin, and most gardens are sheltered behind hedges of gorse and hawthorn. The coast here is broken into coves, sea stacks and shingle beaches that change shape after every storm. Trains on the Ysolde Mountain Tramway reach a top speed of 65 km/h. Drizzle can roll in from the west with very little warning. Most houses in Ysolde Harbour are built of the same stone, roofed with heavy slate. In spring the moorland above Ysolde Harbour turns rough and quiet, and the wind rarely drops. Even on calm days the sound of the surf is never far away. Ysolde is a windswept island of low hills, stone walls and narrow lanes that end abruptly at the sea.

## Staff and volunteers

Shared work, from harvests to roof repairs, still binds the community together. Many households combine several trades, such as stone carving in winter and fishing in summer. The village hall hosts dances, council meetings and the occasional heated debate. Children from the outlying farms travel to school in Merrow End by bus or, in bad weather, not at all. The community council meets in the old chapel, whose benches are famously uncomfortable. Young people often leave for the mainland to study, and some of them eventually return.

There is a strong tradition of dry-stone walling, passed from parents to children at the kitchen table. People in Ysolde Harbour are generally welcoming, if a little reserved with strangers at first. Conversations in Ysolde Harbour often turn to the weather, the ferries and the price of plum cake. Local musicians gather in the back room of the harbour inn most weekends. Sabine Quellmere, who runs the bakery near the harbour, is a reliable source of local gossip. Volunteers from Merrow End give up their weekends to keep the paths and stone walls in repair. A noticeboard outside the shop in Merrow End lists everything from lost dogs to choir rehearsals.

## The route

From the high ground above Merrow End the neighbouring islands look close enough to touch on a clear day. Sheep graze right up to the cliff edge, apparently unconcerned by the drop. There are 16 stations along the Ysolde Mountain Tramway. Peat cutting has left long dark scars across the moor, now softened by heather. Tidal pools along the shore are full of anemones, crabs and small darting fish. Fields are small and irregular, shaped more by the rocks than by any plan. A popular local saying holds that Ysolde has four seasons in a single afternoon.

It has been the subject of friendly rivalry between Ysolde Harbour and the neighbouring parishes. End to end, the Ysolde Mountain Tramway measures 7.4 km. A watercolour of the scene hangs in the reading room of the library in Merrow End. Amateur historians continue to argue about details that matter mostly to themselves. Visiting writers have described it as salt-stained, a word the islanders have happily adopted. There is even a recipe for crab soup that is said to have been named after it.

## Riding the line

Cash is still useful, as not every stall or honesty box accepts cards. Visitors should bring waterproof clothing whatever the forecast says. Visitors are asked to close every gate behind them and to keep to marked paths. There is a small café nearby that serves plum cake and strong tea. Dogs are welcome on a lead, but sheep and ground-nesting birds deserve a wide berth.

## Notes

Island children learn about it long before they can spell its name. Local schoolchildren sometimes choose it as the topic for their end-of-year projects. Some families keep scrapbooks of newspaper cuttings that mention it. Not every story told about it is true, but most of them are entertaining. In Merrow End it is considered bad luck to speak ill of it while at sea. The subject has inspired poems, paintings and at least one rather bad novel. The island newsletter runs a short item about it at least once every autumn.

Tea towels and postcards bearing its image are sold in the harbour shop. Older islanders insist that things were done more carefully in their day. Several folk songs from Merrow End mention it, usually in the last verse. The Saltmere Archipelago is not short of curiosities, yet the railway still stands out. The account below is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date. It is fair to say that the railway has shaped the rhythm of daily life in Ysolde Harbour.
